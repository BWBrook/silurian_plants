## In brief

*Partitatheca* is a genus of minute, charcoalified sporangia from the early Lochkovian of Shropshire. The sporangia split into four valves and hold permanent, sculptured cryptospore dyads (in one specimen, tetrads). It links dispersed *Cymbohilates* cryptospores to a parent plant, and one stem shows a branching, stomata-bearing sporophyte, a combination unknown in living plants (Edwards et al. 2014). It lived some 50 Myr after the first cryptospores, so it shows late survivors of the cryptospore flora, not the Ordovician plants.

## Morphology and anatomy

Observed. The genus was erected for seven quadrivalvate sporangia, selected from several hundred, whose walls have longitudinal files of large cells; four species (*P. splendida* (type), *P. horrida*, *P. densa*, *P. cymosa*) are named from their spinose dyads (Edwards et al. 2012a). Species differ in sporangium shape, valve shape and the presence of stomata, and one may have stayed attached at the apex. Edwards et al. (2014) extended the genus to sporangia with murornate dyads (Morris et al. 2012), laevigate dyads and a single sporangium with laevigate tetrads (Edwards et al. 2012b).

Spore walls are complex. In *Cymbohilates horridus* var. *splendidus*, each dyad member has a homogeneous inner layer, the two are fused proximally, and an outer wall of at least five layers surrounds both; one zone under 40 nm thick shows alternating dark and light lines read as laminae (Edwards et al. 2014). Dyads never separate naturally; dispersed monads are torn apart.

Inferred. Eophyte axes were ridged, rarely over 1-2 mm wide, with food-conducting cells and no tracheids (Edwards et al. 2021a); *Partitatheca* may belong to this group but its own subtending anatomy is almost unknown.

## Age and distribution

One locality: Brown Clee Hill, early Lochkovian, from the Ditton Group, with fossils charred in low-temperature wildfire (Edwards et al. 2014, 2021a). Cryptospores declined sharply around 410 Ma in the latest Lochkovian (Edwards et al. 2021a; on GTS2012, whose Lochkovian top is 410.8 Ma; the same horizon sits near 413 Ma on ICS v2024/12), and dyads vanished from the dispersed record before the Middle Devonian (Edwards et al. 2012a).

## Affinity and controversies

Edwards et al. (2012a) saw wall ultrastructure like the *Dyadospora* complex (read as hepatic) but a branching sporophyte and stomata, and proposed a new embryophyte lineage. Edwards et al. (2014) argued that limited branching and stomata favour a more derived position than hepatics, and that stomata plus dispersed polyads imply distal spore germination in basal stomatophytes. They contrast *Lenticulatheca* (hilate monads, closer to basal tracheophytes). Edwards and Kenrick (2015) held a sister relationship to tracheophytes plausible for some species (as cited by Edwards et al. 2021a). Edwards et al. (2021a) list *Partitatheca* only as a "possible" eophyte, because its axes are poorly known, and allow eophytes to lie on the stem of the liverwort-moss clade or of tracheophytes, diverging before *Aglaophyton*. Edwards et al. (2014) insist cryptophytes are not bryophytes. Hooper et al. (2026) ran their spore-evolution analyses with *Partitatheca* and *Lenticulatheca* as stem-embryophytes and, alternatively, as stem-tracheophytes. Under one model, *Partitatheca*'s enveloped dyads pulled the reconstructed ancestral embryophyte spore towards an enveloped state; they discounted this because its position is not well evidenced, and note that eophytes have not been placed by a formal cladistic analysis. Resolving this needs attached axes with anatomy, and the gametophyte.

## Ecology and physiology

If *Partitatheca* is an eophyte, its size and anatomy imply poikilohydric plants that hydrated from external water and equilibrated rapidly with the environment (Edwards et al. 2021a). Eophyte stomata are sparse and have collapsed guard cells, like moss and hornwort sporophytes, so they probably served sporangium drying and dehiscence, not transpiration (Edwards et al. 2021a). Dyads suit sexual reproduction only if gametophytes were bisexual (Edwards et al. 2014). Whether the sporophyte depended on a gametophyte, as in bryophytes, is speculative. Local displacement by larger zosterophylls in the late Lochkovian is the favoured cause of decline (Edwards et al. 2014; Edwards and Richardson 2004).

## Why it matters for modelling

- Body size: millimetres to c. 2 cm tall (an inference for eophytes), with axes under 2 mm; no water-conducting tissue, so growth tied to surface water and activity in pulses.
- Stomata without a transpiration stream: gas-exchange rules for these plants should not copy tracheophytes.
- Reproduction: permanent dyads or tetrads set mating constraints (selfing needs bisexual gametophytes); sporogenesis always gave dyads or tetrads, indicating strict genetic control (Edwards et al. 2014).
- Rarity: seven sporangia among several hundred means the group was a small fraction of fertile mesofossils at this site.
- Replacement: cryptospores dominated assemblages for c. 60 Myr and then fell sharply near 410 Ma on GTS2012, about 413 Ma on ICS v2024/12 (Edwards et al. 2021a). Displacement by zosterophylls across the late Lochkovian in the Anglo-Welsh Basin is a calibratable event.

## Open questions

- What is the internal anatomy of *Partitatheca* axes, and are they eophytes?
- Did Ordovician dyad-producers share this sporophyte form? Edwards et al. (2014) warn that key features may have evolved later.
- Which gametophyte goes with these sporophytes?
- Were dyads the ancestral cryptospore state, as Edwards et al. (2014) suggest should be considered?

## In brief

Salopella is a form genus of the [rhyniophytoid](rhyniophytoids.md) grade: small, leafless plants whose isotomous axes each end in an elongate, fusiform sporangium. Edwards & Richardson (1974) erected it for Lochkovian material from the Welsh Borderland, and species have since been named from Victoria, north-west China and Brazil. It spans the Pridoli to the Pragian, and spores from Salopella-shaped sporangia in Shropshire differ from one sporangium to another, so it should be treated as a shape category, not a clade.

## Morphology and anatomy

The type species was based on a single specimen (Tims & Chambers 1984; Brown Clee Hill, Lochkovian, according to McSweeney et al. 2021), with true fusiform sporangia of dimensions similar to those of S. australis. The Victorian species are larger fragments. S. australis (Tims & Chambers 1984, emended by McSweeney et al. 2021) has axes 0.9-2.4 mm wide, two parallel parent axes each with one distal dichotomy, and parallel-sided sporangia 6.5-14.0 mm long and 1.3-2.0 mm wide, as wide as their stalks and tapering to a point; at least 80 mm tall. S. caespitosa is a tuft of more than 20 axes (0.7-1.9 mm wide in the holotype) with up to two or three orders of dichotomy and fusiform sporangia 3.2-6.0 by 1.3-2.0 mm; the holotype shows 26 sporangia, and a second specimen from Ghin Ghin Road is 135 mm long and in semi-relief. S. laidae (McSweeney et al. 2021) has at least 55 mm of axis and eight sporangia 9.7-11.0 mm long and up to 2.5 mm wide, wider than their stalks. S. marcensis and S. xinjiangensis have sporangia only about 3.4-3.5 mm long (as summarised by McSweeney et al. 2021). No Victorian species preserves spores or anatomy; a central line in the axes is read as a possible vascular trace.

## Age and distribution

Welsh Borderland: S. allenii and S. marcensis, Lochkovian (Fanning et al. 1992, early Gedinnian near Ludlow), with coalified Salopella-shaped sporangia from Shropshire (Edwards et al. 1994). S. xinjiangensis is Pridoli, dated by graptolites (Cai et al. 1993). In Victoria, S. australis and S. caespitosa come from Frenchmans Spur in the Wilson Creek Shale. Tims & Chambers (1984) also assigned Limestone Road specimens, from the Lower Plant Assemblage they placed in the Ludlow, to S. australis, giving it a Ludlow-Pragian range; McSweeney et al. (2021) removed them (one became the type of S. laidae, the rest indeterminate), so S. australis is no longer known from that assemblage. S. laidae (Limestone Road) and S. caespitosa (Ghin Ghin Road) are. Their age is the unresolved Baragwanathia-flora problem: Earp (2024) finds the western Yea localities Pragian-Emsian and leaves the eastern ones undated; see [the Baragwanathia floras](baragwanathia-flora.md).

The PBDB, following Tims & Chambers's stratigraphy, still codes Victorian S. australis as Ludlow. It also lists S. brasiliana (Paraná Basin; original description, Mussa et al. 1996, not available to me), a Pridoli Welsh Salopella sp. whose source it could not trace, and Argentine 'cf. Salopella'; I did not examine these.

## Affinity and controversies

Edwards & Richardson (1974) assigned Salopella to the Rhyniaceae, and Tims & Chambers (1984) compared the Victorian plants with Rhynia major, Steganotheca and Eogaspesiea, noting sporangial and axial dimensions almost exactly those of R. major. They speculated that the fertile region of S. australis might be little more than part of the shoot apex, and possibly the most primitive sporangial condition known: a hypothesis from gross form.

The spore evidence undermines the genus. Edwards et al. (1994) found in situ Aneurospora-like spores of two ornament types in separate sporangia of S. cf. marcensis, and hilate cryptospores in one other Salopella-shaped sporangium. McSweeney et al. (2021) cite this, with convergence of sporangial shape, to call the genus paraphyletic and to say that palaeogeographic claims based on it need palynological support. Fanning et al. (1992) noted that forking, terminal sporangia without spores might belong to Salopella on spore evidence from elsewhere.

## Ecology and physiology

The Welsh sporangia come from fluvial rocks (Edwards et al. 1994); Victorian plants are drifted fragments. McSweeney et al. (2021) relay the hypothesis that rhyniophytoids were ruderal colonisers of ephemeral ground with short life cycles, and note that the Melbourne Zone lay about 30 degrees south in the Early Devonian on the reconstruction they use. Both are inferences.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Fecundity.** A tufted S. caespitosa individual bore at least 20 fertile axes and about 26 visible sporangia of 3-6 mm; S. australis specimens show about four sporangia of 6.5-14 mm. Observed counts on fragments only.
- **Sporangium as organ.** The proposal that the sporangium is barely differentiated from the axis tip gives a minimal-sporangium state for F1.
- **Height (F2).** Tufts at least 13.5 cm tall (Ghin Ghin Road; the Wilson Creek Shale holotype at least 8.5 cm), far shorter than co-occurring Baragwanathia, consistent with the 'low vegetation alongside' larger zosterophylls of Fanning et al. (1992).
- **Record (F3, F4).** Treat Salopella as a morphotype. Capel et al. (2023) say the Australia-Xinjiang-Greenland cluster in their Silurian-Lochkovian analysis is caused by shared Salopella alone, so it is no dispersal signal. PBDB-coded Ludlow Victorian records should be re-dated.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Contrast fertile branching and sporangial dimensions among small naked plants while testing how convergent organ shapes and revised identifications alter apparent distributions and first appearances.

- **Can transfer:**
  - S. australis, Frenchmans Spur specimens: sporangia 6.5-14.0 mm long and 1.3-2.0 mm wide constrain organ dimensions for the emended species (Tims & Chambers 1984; McSweeney et al. 2021).
  - S. caespitosa, Frenchmans Spur holotype: 26 visible sporangia constrain the preserved fertile architecture, without establishing complete lifetime fecundity (McSweeney et al. 2021).
  - S. caespitosa, Ghin Ghin Road specimen: 135 mm preserved length supplies a minimum size constraint, not measured whole-plant height (McSweeney et al. 2021).
  - S. cf. marcensis, coalified Shropshire sporangia: retain separate Aneurospora-like ornament types in separate sporangia as reproductive observations, without merging them into one organism (Edwards et al. 1994).
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not assign Welsh trilete or hilate spore types to S. australis, S. caespitosa or S. laidae; Victorian species have no recovered in situ spores.
  - Do not interpret different Welsh spore types as heterospory within a Salopella individual.
  - Do not assign tracheids or hydraulic conductance from the Victorian central axial line; its vascular interpretation remains tentative.
  - Do not retain the Limestone Road occurrence as a Ludlow S. australis calibration; the relevant specimens were removed from that species (McSweeney et al. 2021).

## Open questions

What spores do the Victorian species carry? Is S. brasiliana congeneric? Is Pridoli S. xinjiangensis the earliest reliable record? Do any Victorian sporangia preserve cryptospores or trilete spores?

## In brief

The Rhynie chert, with the nearby Windyfield chert, is a hot-spring Konservat-Lagerstatte in Aberdeenshire, Scotland. It preserves at cellular level seven species of land plants, many in growth position, together with fungi from every major lineage except Basidiomycota, algae, cyanobacteria, amoebae, nematophytes and 14 invertebrate species, all arthropods but one nematode (Strullu-Derrien et al. 2019; Krings et al. 2017). It is the main source of anatomical data on early land plants. For an observation model it is one habitat sampled over a short window, with a stage assignment that depends on the dating method.

All seven plants are mostly parenchyma with a small central strand, cuticle and stomata (Kerp 2017). The Windyfield chert adds *Ventarura*, a centipede, a euthycarcinoid and hexapod material (Anderson & Trewin 2003; Fayers & Trewin 2005) and an arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus in *Aglaophyton* (Strullu-Derrien et al. 2025).

## Age and distribution

**Age.** Biostratigraphic (spores) and radiometric; see range notes. The U-Pb and 40Ar/39Ar ages differ by about 4 Myr, more than the 2.4 Myr Pragian.

**Setting.** A fault-bounded intermontane half-graben on the south-east margin of Euramerica, with an axial river and an active hot-spring system at the margin (Wellman 2017). Palaeolatitude about 25 degrees S (Wellman 2017) or 28 degrees S (Edwards et al. 2017); neither source names a plate model. PBDB, which lumps Rhynie into one collection, gives about 5 degrees S (gplates) or 24 degrees S (Scotese), so carry latitude as a range.

**Taphonomy.** Silica sinter from alkali-chloride hot springs; plants rooted on sand and sinter between floods and were silicified by periodic silica-rich inundation (Wellman 2017; Channing 2017). Fluid temperature is disputed: 90-100 degrees C (Rice et al., as cited by Wellman 2017), under 40-50 degrees C from fluid inclusions, or low-temperature pools on the distal apron (Channing 2017) later converted to chert at higher temperature. Plants are often in situ; the "peat" beds are decaying plant accumulations with little bioturbation, not true peat (Edwards et al. 2017).

**Sampling.** Blocks collected by Mackie in 1912; trenches for Kidston and Lang (papers 1917-1921); Lyon's sifting, sectioning and peels (he died in 1999); two trenches in 1964, when some chert was stolen; the Munster group around Remy and Kerp, who cut thousands of thin sections; the first core through the chert-bearing sequence in 1988 and boreholes in 1997-2000. Sectioning was selective (Edwards et al. 2017; Wellman 2017).

## Affinity and controversies

- **Date.** Whether the andesite dates the chert (Parry et al. 2011, 2013) or hydrothermal activity lies near 407 Ma (Mark et al. 2011, 2013; Edwards et al. 2017). Edwards et al. suggest 411.5 Ma may date only the start of volcanism before the cherts.
- **Representativeness.** Channing (2017) argues the plants were pre-adapted to saline, alkaline, metal-rich wetlands and excluded mesophytes. Wellman (2017) finds spores of *Horneophyton*, *Rhynia* and *Aglaophyton* throughout the basin sequence, so they were stress-tolerant ruderals of the wider basin, not hot-spring specialists. Neither shows the chert flora was typical.
- ***Prototaxites*.** Honegger et al. called it an ascomycete (Edwards et al. 2017); Loron et al. (2026) find it chemically and structurally distinct from fungi (see its card).
- **Contamination.** A claim that the mites and springtails are modern contaminants was rejected: silica cannot be remobilised and deep core also holds arthropods (Dunlop & Garwood 2017).

## Ecology and physiology

Plants covered about 40% of the active ground (Powell et al., as reported by Wellman 2017) and formed clonal stands that colonised new sinter or sand and were destroyed by the next flood or silica inundation; no climax community developed. There is no evidence of full submergence, and Kidston and Lang excluded it for *Rhynia* and *Asteroxylon*, which have stomata at the base of upright axes (Wellman 2017). No fish are known, which suggests the pools were hostile (Wellman 2017). Mycorrhiza-like associations occur in at least *Aglaophyton* and *Horneophyton* (Edwards et al. 2017). The animal community looks largely intact apart from some expected groups and resembles the fauna of modern Icelandic hot springs (Dunlop & Garwood 2017). Perhaps 90% of Rhynie fungal species belong to early-diverging lineages that are about 2% of living fungi (Boyce & Nelsen 2025, from Krings et al.).

## Why it matters for modelling

- **One window.** Parry et al. (2011) argue that hot-spring systems stay active for at most tens of thousands of years; Channing (2017) gives tens to hundreds of thousands of years from modern analogues. Tasch's old estimate of 1,000 +/- 200 years for a 3.7 m chert sequence is quoted by Wellman (2017). This is a point sample inside a 2.4 Myr stage, on one habitat.
- **Stage ambiguity.** 411.5 +/- 1.3 Ma (Pragian) against 407.6 +/- 2.2 Ma (Emsian). A model that places Rhynie at a fixed stage should test both.
- **Lateral coverage.** Cherts are under 15% of the thickness of the chert unit; over 85% is fluvial-lacustrine sediment without silicified plants (Wellman 2017). Chert bodies are of limited lateral extent.
- **Taxonomic filter.** Seven plants in the chert against 31 common and 42 rare dispersed spore taxa in 70 counted samples. Only five spore morphotypes can be tied to chert plants (Wellman 2017, table 1; derived), and most are non-diagnostic *Retusotriletes*. The basin spore flora is less diverse than coeval floodplain floras (31 against 46, 38 and 88 common taxa in SW Wales, Gaspe and the Ardennes-Rhenish region).
- **Exclusions.** No bryophytes, which Edwards et al. (2017) call the greatest enigma of the deposit; low preservation of parenchymatous plants; no macrofossil record of the Rhynie plants outside the chert (Wellman 2017). Kerp (2017) notes plants of the wettest habitats are best preserved and drier-substrate plants lose soft tissue.
- **Trait anchors.** Plant height 20-30 cm; gametophytes about a tenth the size of sporophytes (derived, gametophyte card); all homosporous.

## Open questions

Can the chert-bearing beds be dated directly to settle 411.5 against 407.6 Ma? How do the Windyfield and Rhynie cherts relate in time? What lived on the basin floor and uplands that the chert did not capture? Why are bryophytes absent?

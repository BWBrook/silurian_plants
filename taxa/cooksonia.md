## In brief

Cooksonia is the archetypal rhyniophytoid: a few centimetres of leafless, isotomously branching axis, each tip ending in one sporangium of trilete spores. It holds the oldest generally accepted land-plant megafossils (Wenlock, about 432 to 427 Ma) and, in Lochkovian material, direct evidence for tracheids and stomata. The genus is probably unnatural, and its narrowest members were arguably too slender to feed themselves, so it is the main test case for when the sporophyte became a self-sufficient plant.

## Morphology and anatomy

Observed: dichotomous axes, usually two or three bifurcations, with smooth surfaces and terminal sporangia that are discoidal, lentil-shaped or hemispherical, sometimes on a funnel-shaped subtending axis with a rim. Lang's specimens are about 1 to 6 cm long with 25 to 38 µm spores (Edwards and Kenrick 2015). The C. barrandei holotype is nearly 6 cm long, 1 to 1.5 mm wide, with 3 to 5 mm sporangia; the Irish Wenlock axes are only about 0.2 mm wide (Libertín et al. 2018 supplement).

Anatomy exists only where preservation allows. Edwards et al. (1992) found tubes with differential wall thickening in the axes of Lochkovian C. pertoni, confirming Lang's earlier tracheid-like fragments; the charcoalified specimens show a tracheid cast with annular thickenings and stomata (Edwards and Kenrick 2015). Habgood et al. (2002) described the sporangial wall, rim and spores of the same charcoalified material. Silurian compressions show only outlines, so their vascular status is inferred from form and in situ spores. In situ spore types differ among species (Ambitisporites, Aneurospora, Synorisporites, Apiculiretusispora), which suggests that shape-based species are heterogeneous.

## Age and distribution

C. barrandei comes from the middle Sheinwoodian of the Barrandian volcanic archipelago (peri-Gondwana), dated by graptolites to about 432 Ma (Libertín et al. 2018). Edwards and Feehan (1980) reported the first Cooksonia-type sporangia from the uppermost Wenlock of Ireland; Hooper et al. (2026) still use this Irish record as the earliest total-group tracheophyte for molecular-clock calibration. Garza et al. (2024) dated three of the oldest British and Irish sites by detrital zircon (LA-ICP-MS, maximum depositional ages): 426 ± 2 Ma and 427 ± 2 Ma for Ireland and Capel Horeb, 423 ± 3 Ma for Cwm Graig Ddu. These are maxima, not eruption ages, and the paper does not treat the Czech fossils.

By the Přídolí the genus is widespread: the Welsh Borderland, Ontario and New York (Edwards et al. 2004), Bohemia (C. bohemica; Libertín et al. 2022), Podolia, Poland and Libya (Pšenička et al. 2021), with Cooksonia-like plants in Ludlow strata of Bolivia at 50 to 60° S (Morel et al. 1995). C. paranensis (Gerrienne et al. 2001) is early Lochkovian in Brazil (Gerrienne et al. 2006). Spores of land plants predate this first macrofossil by about 40 Myr (Hooper et al. 2026).

## Affinity and controversies

Cooksonia is paraphyletic or polyphyletic: some species sit in the stem group of eutracheophytes (C. pertoni), others in the lycophyte total group (Kenrick et al. 2012; Edwards and Kenrick 2015). Gonez and Gerrienne (2010) restricted the genus by an emended diagnosis and lectotypification, using new C. paranensis specimens and the literature with C. pertoni as type species, and in a companion paper moved C. caledonica to Aberlemnia. C. hemisphaerica sensu stricto is doubtful in north-west Gondwana (Pšenička et al. 2021), C. bohemica remains contested between Cooksonia and Aberlemnia (Libertín et al. 2022), and Edwards et al. (2004) argued that gross simplicity prevents specific assignment in some collections. Treat "Cooksonia" in any dataset as a morphotype, not a clade.

## Ecology and physiology

Boyce (2008) argued that once desiccation resistance, support and conduction are accommodated, axes of the smaller species leave too little volume for aerated photosynthetic tissue, implying dependence on an unpreserved gametophyte; Edwards and Kenrick (2015) summarise this as probably poikilohydric and not photosynthetically competent for the narrow species. Libertín et al. (2018) counter that the 1 to 1.5 mm C. barrandei was large enough for photosynthetic autonomy, a size inference without preserved anatomy. Both are inferences from size. Gerrienne et al. (2006) read one large C. paranensis specimen, five axes on a small thalloid base, as sporophytes on a small gametophyte, which would make the life cycle heteromorphic; it is one fossil.

## Why it matters for modelling

- Treat axis width as the state variable for sporophyte autonomy: about 0.2 mm (probably dependent) to 1.5 mm (arguably independent) within the same morphogenus.
- Sporangium to spore scaling: 3 to 5 mm sporangium with 10 to 15 µm spores in C. barrandei; 18 to 38 µm spores elsewhere; no spore counts exist.
- Timing: macrofossil first appearance 432 to 427 Ma, against dispersed trilete spores from the Late Ordovician; the macrofossil record probably under-samples earlier plants (inference).
- Latitudinal spread: about 20° S (Poland, Přídolí; Pšenička et al. 2021) to 50 to 60° S (Bolivia, Ludlow; Morel et al. 1995).
- Stomata present but sparse; tracheids only demonstrated from the Lochkovian.

## Open questions

Is C. barrandei vascular, and does the Czech record displace the Irish one? Can charcoalified Silurian material be found to test Boyce's argument directly? Which Cooksonia-like species survive redefinition? Are the C. paranensis gametophyte-like structures real?

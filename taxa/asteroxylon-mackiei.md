## In brief

Asteroxylon mackiei, from the Rhynie chert of Scotland, is the largest and most complex plant in that flora: a lycopsid with three distinct axis types (leafy shoots, root-bearing axes, rooting axes) and vein-less leaf-like enations. It is the key fossil for the stepwise assembly of leaves and roots in the lycophyte lineage. Its rooting axes lack root caps and root hairs, so they are transitional between the rootless ancestors and the true roots of later lycopsids, which supports an independent origin of roots in lycophytes and euphyllophytes (Hetherington & Dolan 2018).

## Morphology and anatomy

Hetherington et al. (2021) built the first evidence-based reconstruction from 31 serial thick sections of one in situ plant (4.8 x 3.5 x 2.8 cm) and a 119-peel series through a branch point. A horizontal leafy shoot about 1 cm wide carried thinner (about 6 mm) leafy branches, some near-vertical, and root-bearing axes (about 2-3.5 mm) that grew straight down; these gave rise to rooting axes under 2 mm wide (often under 1 mm) that were only weakly gravitropic and branched more than four times within 1 cm. The reconstructed height of about 30 cm comes from older reconstructions that combined several individuals.

The leaf-like appendages reach 5 mm and are not vascularised: leaf traces leave the central stele and end abruptly just before the leaf base, often marked by dark clumps of cells (Kerp 2018). Stomata (50-70 µm long) are sunken and occur on rhizomes and aerial axes but not on leaves, sporangia or rooting axes. The stele is a simple strand in rhizomes and an actinostele in upright stems, with G-type tracheids and a three-zoned cortex but no endodermis (Kerp 2018). The tissue formerly called phloem is now read as food-conducting cells larger than true phloem cells, with no pericycle and with putative sieve pores, the earliest known (Cooper & Hetherington 2026). Sporangia are kidney-shaped, two-valved and stalked, sit among the leaf-like appendages, and release trilete spores 44-59 µm across, with fertile and sterile zones alternating along an axis (Kerp et al. 2013; Kerp 2018).

Rooting axes had a continuous epidermis over a multicellular promeristem and no root cap in five apices found among 641 thin sections (Hetherington & Dolan 2018). They formed from root-bearing axes by anisotomous dichotomy, with the xylem trace forking, rather than endogenously (Hetherington et al. 2021).

## Age and distribution

Asteroxylon is known only from the Rhynie chert, so its range is the age of that deposit (see range notes): spores say middle Pragian to early Emsian, and U-Pb (411.5 Ma) and 40Ar/39Ar (407.6 Ma) ages differ by about 4 Myr. The link between the dated andesite and the hot springs is unproven (Edwards et al. 2018), so the older age may date only the inception of volcanism. Dating of the biostratigraphic frame is strong; the numerical age is not.

## Affinity and controversies

Placement as a drepanophycalean lycopsid is widely used. Li & Edwards (1995) concluded that Drepanophycus qujingensis is closest to Asteroxylon and that both show the fertile organisation expected in lycopsid ancestors, and Hueber (1992) described its apical meristem for comparison with Lycopodium. Claisse et al. (2025) recover Asteroxylon and Drepanophycus together as a lycopsid clade sister to the Sawdoniaceae. Whether the enations were true microphylls is contested: Kerp (2018) says non-vascularised appendages 'cannot be termed leaves', whereas Hetherington et al. (2021) use the word freely. On roots, Hetherington et al. derive rooting axes from branches, whereas Matsunaga & Tomescu (2016), working on a Wyoming lycophyte, argue that lycophyte roots arose as a novel organ rather than from shoot branches; the two views are not yet reconciled (reviewed by Hetherington & Dolan 2017).

## Ecology and physiology

Asteroxylon is the only Rhynie plant without rhizoids (Kerp 2018), its rooting axes bear no root hairs, and a chytrid-like fungus, Palaeozoosporites renaultii, colonised the inner cortex of its rooting system and was probably parasitic, whereas most Rhynie plants had mycorrhiza-type associations (Strullu-Derrien et al. 2015). How it took up water and nutrients is therefore unresolved. Rhynie plants share stomatal features suggesting high water-use efficiency (Edwards et al. 1998). Growth was by horizontal leafy shoots that gave orthotropic branches, with periodic fertile zones.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Architecture.** Three compartments with measured diameters (leafy shoot 6-10 mm, root-bearing 2-3.5 mm, rooting under 2 mm) and a branching rule (anisotomous dichotomy, K-branching for root-bearing axes).
- **Foliage.** Leaf-like appendages at most 5 mm, sparse on plagiotropic axes and dense on upright ones, with no veins; light capture was by axes plus small enations, with no leaf hydraulics.
- **Belowground function.** Roots lacked caps and hairs and there was no mycorrhiza, so soil exploration and uptake were probably poorer than in later lycopsids; a model should not give Asteroxylon true-root uptake properties.
- **Reproduction.** Homosporous, with 44-59 µm trilete spores, kidney-shaped sporangia 0.6-5.6 mm wide, and growth periodicity.
- **Timing.** The 4 Myr dating gap changes whether the three-axis body plan appears in the Pragian or the Emsian.
- **Transport.** Xylem is a small fraction of stem area (Kerp 2018) and the food-conducting tissue was not true phloem (Cooper & Hetherington 2026), which constrain long-distance transport rates.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Contrast vascular, rhizomatous growth and lateral reproduction with incomplete root and leaf differentiation, using Rhynie specimens that preserve distinct shoot, root-bearing and rooting axes.

- **Can transfer:**
  - A. mackiei, Rhynie chert in situ reconstruction: separate downward root-bearing axes about 2-3.5 mm wide from rooting axes under 2 mm, often under 1 mm (Hetherington et al. 2021).
  - A. mackiei, Rhynie chert rooting apices: represent root-cap absence observed in five apices among 641 thin sections (Hetherington & Dolan 2018).
  - A. mackiei, Rhynie chert appendages: use a maximum length of 5 mm and absence of vascularisation as foliage geometry and anatomy (Kerp 2018).
  - A. mackiei, Rhynie chert fertile axes: use trilete spores 44-59 µm across and alternating fertile and sterile zones (Kerp et al. 2013; Kerp 2018).
  - A. mackiei, Rhynie chert stem sections: represent G-type tracheids, a three-zoned cortex and absence of an endodermis as anatomical states (Kerp 2018).
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not treat about 30 cm as measured whole-plant height; the older reconstruction combines several individuals.
  - Do not assign later lycopsid true-root uptake properties to the capless, hairless rooting axes; their uptake function remains unresolved.
  - Do not model the observed rooting-system fungus as a demonstrated nutritional mutualist; this card interprets it as probably parasitic.
  - Do not assign true-phloem transport parameters to the food-conducting tissue; this card distinguishes it from true phloem.

## Open questions

Is the 411.5 Ma or the 407.6 Ma age correct for the hot-spring chert? Does any Asteroxylon leaf contain vascular tissue? How were nutrients taken up without hairs or mycorrhiza? What is the gametophyte? Do orthotropic and fertile axes connect to the reconstructed body? Do other localities hold Asteroxylon-grade plants?

## In brief

*Polytrichum commune* is a large, desiccation-tolerant moss whose gametophyte has a central strand of water-conducting hydroids, has stems usually 5-10 cm long and up to 70 cm, and carries a sporophyte that stays nourished by the gametophyte. In a Tasmanian population, its hydroid shape showed no significant change down to about -1.45 MPa, which the authors read as no buckling under tension, half of the cavitation signal had accrued by about -1.8 MPa, and hydroids refilled after rewetting; leaf diffusive conductance fell sharply before major cavitation (Brodribb et al. 2020). The same study found that photosynthesis, not plumbing, is the weak point: leaves have no stomata and exchange water for CO2 poorly. The moss separates hydraulic capability from sporophyte dominance, since a gametophyte with internal conduction and regulated transpiration is still the dominant generation and still has a dependent sporophyte.

## Morphology and anatomy

Observed in *P. commune* (Brodribb et al. 2020): the stem carries a central hydrome strand; water-filled hydroids are identified in X-ray tomography by large lumina, thick walls and a paired arrangement, and counted at more than 50 per stem in cryo-SEM sections. Leaves carry photosynthetic lamellae on the upper surface; the lamellae are capped by enlarged, wax-covered terminal cells, and on mild dehydration the margins curl inward, the cells close together and the leaves swing to lie against the stem. Below ground are rhizomes and rhizoids. No hydroid diameter or length was obtained.

Reported for the moss group or its relatives, not measured on *P. commune*: hydroids are imperforate, lose their plasmodesmata at maturity and in polytrichopsid leafy shoots have thin, hydrolysed-looking wall areas among thickened ones that are not secondary walls; they lack lignified secondary walls and xylan epitopes, and are an apomorphy of peristomate mosses, not homologous with xylem (Ligrone et al. 2012, summarising earlier work). Leptoids, the food-conducting cells, occur in both generations of peristomate mosses and in polytrichalean mosses lose their nuclei (Ligrone et al. 2012). In the Polytrichaceae examined (*Atrichum*, *Polytrichastrum*) the seta holds its own strand of hydroids and leptoids running to the spore sac; the *Polytrichastrum* apophysis carries stomata over substomatal cavities, about 100 in *P. ohiensis*, whereas *Atrichum* has none (Renzaglia et al. 2020). *Polytrichum* capsules have up to 250 stomata and *Pogonatum* none (same source, genus level). Flora figures quoted second-hand: seta 5-9 cm, capsule 3-6 mm, spores 5-8(-12) micrometres.

## Age and distribution

Living range: temperate and boreal Northern Hemisphere (see regions). There is no fossil in the window; see the range notes for the Cretaceous Polytrichaceae and the two doubtful older records. Bippus et al. (2018, as relayed by Ignatov & Maslova 2021) found *Eopolytrichum* nested within *Polytrichum*, so the Campanian fossil (about 80 Ma, Konopka et al. 1997) is a calibration for the *Polytrichum* + *Eopolytrichum* clade. The oldest unequivocal Polytrichaceae (136 Ma) is far younger than any clock estimate for the moss crown (420-364 Ma and older) or for Polytrichopsida (297-471 Ma, Laenen et al. as reported by Bippus et al. 2017), and Bippus et al. question the calibration behind the younger end of the Polytrichopsida range. The gap is therefore a measure of fossil absence, and of uncertainty in the clocks, not of the length of an undocumented hydroid lineage.

## Affinity and controversies

Position. Polytrichopsida is the basalmost moss clade with hydroids (Cox et al. 2010 and Ligrone & Duckett 2011, as cited by Ligrone et al. 2012), and Kodner & Graham (2001) call *Polytrichum* an early-divergent moss. Harris et al. (2022) resolve bryophytes and tracheophytes as monophyletic sister groups that diverged 515-494 Ma (abstract), both derived from a more complex ancestor, with bryophytes losing gene families associated with vasculature and stomata and crown mosses dated 420-364 Ma (main text). That paper does not trace the origin of hydroid cells; their separate origin from xylem rests on the anatomical argument of Ligrone et al. (2012).

Do hydroids resist tension or collapse? Brodribb et al. (2020) report no buckling: conduit circularity in 14 *P. commune* plants was not significantly correlated with water potential (water-filled r = 0.42, air-filled r = 0.46, both P > 0.1) down to -1.45 MPa, and they propose that hydroid walls may contain some lignin or a related polymer. Ligrone et al. (2012) argue the opposite for a relative: without rigid lignified walls the hydroids collapse reversibly under water stress, which avoids cavitation, and refill and expand on rehydration (their Fig. 11, *Polytrichastrum formosum*). The two accounts differ in species, and Ligrone et al. give no water-potential values in the text I read; neither study tested the other's species. A non-significant correlation in 14 plants is not evidence of zero deformation; the data show no detectable trend, which is weaker than the authors' reading (my assessment).

Why mosses stay short. Brodribb et al. (2020) conclude that the barrier is not water transport or regulation of transpiration but an inefficient water-for-CO2 exchange ratio in stomata-free leaves, and rank cuticle, stomata on photosynthetic surfaces and high mesophyll conductance above vascular tissue as the key vascular-plant innovations. They list other proposed constraints, namely swimming sperm and absent lignified support, without testing them. McAdam et al. (2021) make the same case from stomata. Ligrone et al. (2012) offer a different reading: hydroids evolved as a way to combine desiccation tolerance with large body size.

## Ecology and physiology

Hydraulics, all on *P. commune* from the Mt Wellington population (Brodribb et al. 2020). Slabs with more than 500 shoots were kept in a misting glasshouse at 10-25 °C and natural photoperiod. Conditions differed by assay, and the four responses below are separate measurements, not one curve.

- Conduit shape (cryo-SEM of bench-dried shoots, 14 plants, more than 50 hydroids per stem): circularity of water-filled and air-filled hydroids was not significantly correlated with water potential (r = 0.42 and 0.46, both P > 0.1), and liquid water persisted in hydroid lumina to -1.45 MPa. Mean circularity stayed high; the authors read this as no buckling.
- Embolism (three imaging methods): the text puts onset below -0.5 MPa; in one stem the optical record shows a burst between -1.45 and -1.85 MPa. Optical P50 was -1.8 MPa (Fig. 2b legend: 9 individuals; Methods: ten replicates). The optical metric is the cumulative area of image change from cavitation events, expressed as a percentage of the total in the sequence, and was recorded on stems drying under the microscope at about 22 °C and 60% relative humidity; the water potential was not measured there but estimated from a separately fitted regression on leaf angle. Cryo-SEM agreed, and X-ray tomography (12 whole plants) located cavitation in hydroids between -1 and -1.9 MPa.
- Hydraulic conductance (excised shoots transpiring at 22 °C, 50% relative humidity and 1500 micromol photons per m2 per s, per projected leaf area): shoot conductance fell rapidly between -1 and -2 MPa (18 plants in the figure legend, 20 in Methods), matching the embolism range. Read from Extended Data Figs 2 and 3, approximate: shoot about 10-11 and rhizome about 10 mmol per m2 per s per MPa (7 plants, whole plants measured at 22 °C and 75% relative humidity), with resistance split about 52:48 between shoot and rhizome (the caption says only that the split is similar), and shoot conductance about 11-15 at full hydration. The paper's text gives none of these as numbers.
- Refilling: in a single stem dried for over 12 h after the last cavitation, with both leaves and rhizome rewetted, hydroids began to refill about 90 min after rewetting and the remaining air was compressed to nanobubbles by about 125 min.
- Efficiency: the ratio of maximum assimilation to shoot conductance (7 plants) fell close to the vascular-plant relationship (Extended Data Fig. 1).

Gas exchange (shoots in a cuvette at 22 °C and 1500 micromol photons per m2 per s). Leaf diffusive conductance, as high as the highest angiosperm values when hydrated, fell to under 15% of maximum by -1.5 MPa, before major cavitation; leaves also moved to lie against the stem. Without regulation the model predicts hydroid cavitation below 80% relative humidity at 25 °C, yet plants held assimilation above zero down to 10% at 22 °C (50 plants). Assimilation approached zero at a vapour pressure deficit of 2.5 kPa. Intrinsic water-use efficiency (assimilation per unit conductance) was far below that of lycophytes (20 species) and ferns (64 species) over a similar assimilation range, and conductance stayed high when assimilation was zero. The 100 plants used for the dehydration series came from the larger, more emergent plants of the colony.

Sporophyte nutrition. In *Polytrichum formosum* (not *P. commune*; Renault et al. 1992, abstract only) the sporophyte depends on the gametophyte for carbon, "especially" in *Polytrichum* species. Sucrose was the main soluble sugar in both generations, up to about 230 mM in the haustorium, whereas sugars in the vaginula apoplast were mostly hexoses; a cell-wall invertase peaked in the vaginula, glucose uptake by transfer cells was carrier-mediated, and sucrose was re-formed in the haustorium, so sucrose is not continuous across the interface. At the bryophyte gametophyte-sporophyte interface, transfer cells pump solutes and water follows passively, and the moss sporophyte's transient basal meristem lets a continuous vascular strand join foot to sporangium (Ligrone et al. 2012). No fraction of sporophyte carbon from its own photosynthesis was found in the sources I could read, for any *Polytrichum*. For other mosses, capsule 14CO2 uptake relative to the gametophyte was about 1:15 in *Mnium hornum*, 1:35 in *Pleuridium acuminatum* and about 1:1 in *Funaria hygrometrica*, and expanded capsules supplied about 20%, 10% and 50% of their own growth assimilate (Proctor 1977, abstract only). Kubásek et al. (2021, abstract only) measured capsule gas exchange in 11 moss species, report that moss sporophytes are effectively homoiohydric with stomata that do not respond to light or CO2, and argue that numerous stomata give some sporophytes higher photosynthetic autonomy. The full text is closed-access and was not read; a later cross-check reports that it includes *P. commune* capsules, and I use no value from it. Dependence, photosynthetic contribution and water regulation are separate variables for the sporophyte.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **F1 (sporophyte takeover).** *P. commune* shows that a gametophyte can have internal water transport, regulated transpiration and stems of 5-70 cm while its sporophyte stays small and nutritionally dependent. Hydraulic capability of the gametophyte therefore does not by itself select for sporophyte dominance; any F1 rule that links the two needs another driver.
- **F2 (height race).** The measured limit is carbon gain per unit water lost, not conduit failure: P50 -1.8 MPa and a conductance-to-photosynthetic-capacity ratio close to vascular plants, but assimilation near zero at 2.5 kPa. A height model for early plants can set hydraulic capacity and water-for-carbon exchange as separate gates, with the second tied to cuticle, stomata and mesophyll conductance (Brodribb et al. 2020). One moss in one population shows this; it does not test what other stomata-free tissue would do.
- **Scale.** Typical stem length of 5-10 cm overlaps the few-centimetre height of the oldest vascular sporophytes (a few centimetres for the Silurian *Cooksonia*-type plant, Ligrone et al. 2012, citing Edwards & Feehan 1980; see [Cooksonia](cooksonia.md)), but belongs to the gametophyte generation, in a plant with no leaf stomata, and stem length is not vertical height.
- **Safety margin.** Leaf conductance is below 15% of maximum by -1.5 MPa, after the -0.5 MPa cavitation onset and close to the -1.8 MPa P50; the margin is thin, which is why photosynthesis is humidity-sensitive (my reading of the same data).
- **Fungal axis.** Not covered here; the moss was not examined for fungal associations in the sources read. For the fungal-nutrition contrast see [Haplomitrium gibbsiae](haplomitrium-gibbsiae.md) and [Marchantia polymorpha](marchantia-polymorpha.md).

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Water-transport, sporophyte-independence and height: a gametophyte with internal hydroid conduction and regulated transpiration, still dominant, bearing a dependent sporophyte, set against the non-conducting thalloid liverwort [Marchantia polymorpha](marchantia-polymorpha.md) and against stomatal, lignified, sporophyte-dominant tracheophytes.

- **Can transfer:** (all *P. commune* from one Mt Wellington, Tasmania, population kept in a glasshouse, from Brodribb et al. 2020, unless stated; keep each as its own relationship)
  - Hydroid shape: no significant trend in conduit circularity with water potential down to -1.45 MPa (cryo-SEM, bench-dried shoots, 14 plants). Use as 'no buckling detected to -1.45 MPa', not as proof of zero deformation.
  - Embolism: onset below -0.5 MPa, optical P50 -1.8 MPa (9 or 10 shoots, drying at about 22 °C and 60% relative humidity, water potential inferred from leaf angle), X-ray tomography cavitation between -1 and -1.9 MPa (12 plants). Use as an embolism-versus-water-potential relationship for a hydroid-bearing gametophyte, with the proxy water potential in mind.
  - Hydraulic conductance: shoot conductance falls rapidly between -1 and -2 MPa (excised shoots, 22 °C, 50% relative humidity, 1500 micromol photons per m2 per s; 18-20 plants), about 10 mmol per m2 per s per MPa per leaf area, with shoot and rhizome each carrying roughly half of the resistance (7 plants; values read from figures, approximate).
  - Refilling: hydroids began to refill about 90 min after rewetting in one stem (leaves and rhizome wetted). Use only as an order-of-magnitude recovery time, not as a rate.
  - Gas exchange (cuvette, 22 °C, 1500 micromol photons per m2 per s): leaf conductance under 15% of maximum by -1.5 MPa, assimilation near zero at about 2.5 kPa, water-use efficiency far below that of 84 lycophyte and fern species. Use as the measured response of this moss, not as a general stomata-free penalty.
  - Stem length (2-)5-10(-70) cm for erect to decumbent stems from the Flora of North America (second-hand); not a Tasmanian measurement.
  - *P. formosum* (named *Polytrichum formosum* by Renault et al. 1992), site unspecified, abstract only: sugar passes from gametophyte to sporophyte through a transfer-cell interface with invertase conversion, sucrose reaching about 230 mM in the haustorium. Use only as a qualitative statement of dependence, labelled *P. formosum*.
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not merge embolism, conductance loss and refilling into one vulnerability curve: they are separate assays under different conditions, and only the first two are tied to water potential measured or inferred.
  - Do not read stem length as vertical height: stems are erect to decumbent and sometimes forked, and no height was measured for the Tasmanian plants.
  - Do not report the hydroids as non-deforming: the circularity result is non-significant in 14 plants.
  - Do not carry *P. formosum* sugar-transfer values to *P. commune* or the reverse; the hydroid-collapse images (Ligrone et al. 2012) are also *Polytrichastrum formosum*, not *P. commune*.
  - Do not treat hydroids as xylem or as an early tracheophyte conducting cell: they are imperforate, of independent origin and lack lignified secondary walls (Ligrone et al. 2012); and do not assume the P50 applies to other moss hydroids, including the tall *Dawsonia* (Bok et al. 2022 measured its hydroid widening, abstract only, not vulnerability).
  - Do not take any sporophyte carbon fraction for *Polytrichum* from this card: none was found in the sources read, and the 10-50% values of Proctor (1977) belong to *Mnium hornum*, *Pleuridium acuminatum* and *Funaria hygrometrica*. *P. commune* capsule gas exchange may be in the closed-access Kubásek et al. (2021).
  - Do not use the stomata trait for leaves: the capsule has stomata, the leaves have none, and the count (up to 250) is genus-level.
  - Do not use a single colony as a population mean: the measurements come from one population, and the dehydration series was sampled from the more emergent plants.
  - Do not use 'mosses lack conducting tissue' as a model default: Polytrichopsida shows otherwise, whereas Sphagnopsida and Andreaeopsida lack internal water-conducting cells (Ligrone et al. 2012).
  - Analogy to Palaeozoic plants is bounded, not barred. A model may use *P. commune* as a functional analogy for what a non-lignified, imperforate-celled, stomata-free, desiccation-tolerant gametophyte can do hydraulically (P50 near -1.8 MPa, photosynthesis limited by water-for-CO2 exchange). It may not take the moss as an ancestor or sister of any Palaeozoic plant, take hydroid-xylem homology, import Palaeozoic CO2 or temperature responses (none were tested), or carry sporophyte traits from it. The oldest unequivocal Polytrichaceae fossil (136 Ma) fixes a minimum age for the family, not its origin, and bryophytes lost vasculature-associated genes (Harris et al. 2022), so neither fossil minima nor gene loss rules the analogy out; they limit what it can claim.

## Open questions

Sporophyte carbon budget in *P. commune* itself (own photosynthesis, transfer rate, seta and capsule growth), and the species-level capsule stomata number and gas exchange (closed-access Kubásek et al. 2021); whether hydroids collapse or resist in the same species under the same range; hydroid dimensions and wall chemistry, including any lignin-like polymer; vertical height in the Tasmanian population; spore output and fertilisation distance (not found); fungal associations; whether the Late Ordovician peat-moss-like fragments and the Silurian-Devonian tubular microfossils are mosses.

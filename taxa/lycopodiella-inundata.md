## In brief

*Lycopodiella inundata*, the marsh clubmoss, is a homosporous lycophyte of seasonally flooded, nutrient-poor heath and lake margins, with a creeping rhizomatous sporophyte, one erect strobilus per fertile stem and a short-lived but overwintering gametophyte. It earns a card for one experimental series. Hoysted et al. (2019, 2021) traced carbon, phosphorus and nitrogen between plants collected at Thursley (Surrey) and their fungus, at three sporophyte stages. Every sample examined so far carries Mucoromycotina fine root endophytes (MFRE; several sequence types) and no Glomeromycotina. Fungal function changed with plant stage: no detectable carbon allocation to the fungus and no nitrogen return in juvenile sporophytes, abundant exchange in mature adults, and weaker exchange in retreating adults. The gametophyte was not tested; its nutrition is inferred from other Lycopodiaceae. The only species-specific description I read (Bruce 1972) shows a green surface gametophyte, which conflicts with the "subterranean, achlorophyllous" description used by Hoysted et al.

## Morphology and anatomy

**Sporophyte.** A perennial with annual rhizomatous stem extension, roots with copious long root hairs (100-1000 micrometres long, 20 micrometres wide), a single terminal cone-like strobilus emerging from the stem in late summer, spread by creeping axes and vegetative fragmentation (Kowal et al. 2020, partly relaying Byfield and Stewart). The juvenile sporophyte used in the tracer work had up to seven leaves, protocorm remnants, copious rhizoids and no or rarely one rootlet, and was already detached from the gametophyte (Hoysted et al. 2021). Plant dimensions were not found in a readable source.

**Gametophyte.** Sources disagree, and the species-specific primary descriptions favour a green surface form.
- Bruce (1972), read through partly garbled OCR, reports the first North American gametophytes of *L. inundata*: more than 100 at a Midland County site and about 25 in Van Buren County, Michigan, many with attached young sporophytes up to 1 cm long. The gametophyte is usually a small upright cylinder whose distal expansion carries few to many planate lobes one to several cells thick, giving a bushy look in large specimens. Sex organs lie near the lobe bases, the lower region bears numerous non-septate rhizoids, and the endophytic fungus is confined to that basal region in healthy gametophytes. Sizes vary without a break, and some large ones bear up to five young sporophytes. Some gametophytes survived winter in a mature state under ice and snow, so survival over more than one season is possible. Gametophytes with no attached sporophyte looked healthy, and Bruce suggests that fertilisation and embryo development may lead to gametophyte death. Bruce attributes the first description to Goebel (1887, not read) and places the species among the green, superficial type of Bruchmann's five gametophyte categories, the type he calls characteristic of the bog clubmosses. Bruchmann's type IV is named for *L. inundata* in Rimgaile-Voicik and Naujalis (2022), who list the type without describing it.
- Testo et al. (2018) say Lycopodielloideae and *Phylloglossum* have partly or wholly superficial, photosynthetic, fast-growing gametophytes, unlike the subterranean ones of the rest of the family (in a discussion of substitution rates). In culture, *L. lateralis* spores germinate quickly in light, the young gametophyte is a solid green tubercle, and the mature one is a crown of photosynthetic lobes; a shaft raises a buried young gametophyte towards the surface (Whittier and Renzaglia 2005; abstract only). *L. prostrata* is also photosynthetic but germinated slowly: first germination at two months, 61 of 10,000 spores (0.6%) at one year (Whittier and Carter 2007; partial text). These are congeners.
- Hoysted et al. (2021) call the *L. inundata* gametophytes subterranean, achlorophyllous and mycoheterotrophic, citing Winther and Friedman (2008), whose abstract covers *Huperzia* and *Lycopodium*, not *Lycopodiella*. They give no description of the Thursley gametophytes in the text I read (Hoysted et al. 2019 show gametophyte sections in a supplementary figure I did not see).
The reading best supported by direct description is a green, lobed, surface-living gametophyte that is fungus-colonised at its base; whether it is ever mycoheterotrophic, or buried while young, is untested for this species. Goebel (1887) is the first-hand description to read.

## Age and distribution

Northern hemisphere wet habitats, in Britain and Europe mostly seasonally inundated heathland (Kowal et al. 2020). On the clock of Testo et al. (2018) the Lycopodiaceae crown is 368.4 Ma under the better-fitting model (and 308.8 Ma under the alternative; see range notes), the Lycopodioideae-Lycopodielloideae split is 293.6 Ma (262-320), and Lycopodielloideae radiated out of Oceania in the late Mesozoic, with a north-temperate affinity in *Lycopodiella*. The lycophyte-euphyllophyte split in the same study is 431.5 Ma (426-440). Ages are on the source's timescale.

## Affinity and controversies

Placement in Lycopodiaceae is not in doubt. Four points are disputed or unsettled.
- **Partner identity.** *L. inundata* is the only lycopod reported with MFRE alone (Hoysted et al. 2021, citing Rimington et al. 2015, not read). Hoysted et al. (2019) found Mucoromycotina (Endogonales) and no Glomeromycotina sequences in wild and experimental plants, and Rimington et al. (2020) list it as Mucoromycotina-only. The sequences are several operational taxonomic units shared with neighbouring plants, not one isolated strain, so "one partner" is not established.
- **Gametophyte.** See Morphology and anatomy.
- **Seasonality of colonisation.** Kowal et al. (2020) found 14% of roots colonised in spring against 86% in autumn. They report that an earlier single-site study (Fuchs and Haselwandter, not read) found the opposite, and suggest small sample size, no separation of FRE from arbuscular fungi and no molecular identification as reasons.
- **No transfer is not no function.** The accepted manuscript of Hoysted et al. (2021) says that failing to detect carbon transfer by juveniles does not mean none occurs.

## Ecology and physiology

**Measured exchange, by stage.** All values are from field-collected plants incubated in microcosms in controlled-environment chambers (Hoysted et al. 2019, 2021). The fungus is MFRE in every case. In the 2019 design the nutrient tracers (33P-orthophosphate, 15N-ammonium chloride) were added to a core, and plants were sampled at the peak of above-ground tracer activity 21 days later; the 14CO2 pulse that traced carbon to the hyphae ran for about 16 hours, after that nutrient window. The two tracer windows differ, so the fractions are not paired.

The n values give carbon, 33P and 15N respectively. The 2019 means come from a different batch (n = 20 for carbon, 10 for nutrients) and are not stage-resolved. The sporophyte stages are from the 2021 accepted manuscript, which I read in full (published version not opened). Stage covaries with season, size, photosynthetic tissue and colonisation anatomy (juveniles, summer adults and spring retreating adults), so these comparisons do not isolate a stage effect.

**Field isotopes (Hoysted et al. 2019, Thursley).** *L. inundata* leaves (n = 6) were depleted in 13C relative to *Juncus bulbosus* and to arbuscular, ericoid and ectomycorrhizal reference plants (n = 17), and leaves and roots were enriched in 15N relative to the reference plants. The authors read the 15N enrichment as fungal nitrogen gain; the enrichment is correlative.

**Shared fungus.** The same MFRE sequences occurred in neighbouring plants: the liverwort *Fossombronia foveolata*, *Juncus bulbosus* and *Molinia caerulea* at Thursley and Norfolk (Hoysted et al. 2019). Shared networks, hence possible plant-to-plant flow, are plausible; no plant-to-plant transfer was measured.

**Phenology.** Strobili emerge in late summer; spores give diminutive gametophytes from late summer to spring (Kowal et al. 2020), and some gametophytes overwinter (Bruce 1972). Root colonisation across 1,305 roots from 275 plants at 11 heaths was 14% in spring (2.4% with vesicles) and 86% in autumn (7.6% with vesicles); every colonised root had FRE in cortical cells and root hairs, and colonisation may peak at strobilus formation (Kowal et al. 2020). The survey used new subplots at each of the two dates in one year and scored presence or absence, so it is prevalence, not a seasonal multiplier of nutrient flux. Hoysted et al. report much reduced colonisation in retreating plants, which they collected in spring. The two observations agree in direction (inference; different metrics).

**Gametophyte to sporophyte.** In green-gametophyte lycophytes (*Lycopodiella*, *Palhinhaea*) embryo matrotrophy is described as months, and in *Lycopodium obscurum* with a subterranean gametophyte as up to several years (Renzaglia and Whittier 2013). Both are discussion-level statements: that paper examined collected young sporophytes microscopically, did not time dependence, and takes the figure of up to about four years from a study of another species (*L. digitatum*; Bruce 1979, via the extractor I used). For *L. inundata* the juvenile stage is hypothesised to be partly mycoheterotrophic because the protocorm is colonised and mostly subterranean, with only leaf tips above ground; the juveniles had no hyphal link to mature plants (Hoysted et al. 2021, a hypothesis).

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Stage-dependent trade.** The data support a rule in which a plant's carbon payment to a fungus, and the fungus's return in N and P, vary with developmental stage and not only with the fungus's identity or plant size. Juveniles with leaves, and so with potential photosynthesis, paid no detectable carbon. Stage is confounded with season and tissue here (see above), so this is a hypothesis-generating pattern.
- **Nitrogen weighting.** With MFRE alone, more 15N than 33P tracer reached mature adults (Hoysted et al. 2019, absolute P = 0.05, per biomass P = 0.03). This tests whether a Rhynie-type partnership should be modelled as phosphorus-weighted by default (see [Early glomeromycotan fungi](early-glomeromycotan-fungi.md)).
- **Seasonal pulse.** Root colonisation varied several-fold within a year (14% to 86% of roots), as prevalence.
- **Pre-root uptake.** Juveniles with rhizoids and no root received 33P (but not 15N) from the fungus, a contrast for rootless hosts.
- **Sporophyte independence as a staged process.** In this lineage independence runs through a fungus-colonised protocorm attached to a green, short-lived gametophyte, and nutrition by the fungus changes with stage.
- **Gradient of dependence.** Embryo matrotrophy is described as years in *Lycopodium* and months in green-gametophyte *Lycopodiella*; see also [Ceratopteris richardii](ceratopteris-richardii.md) and [Selaginella](selaginella.md) for the other end.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** fungal-nutrition and sporophyte-independence, because it is the living vascular plant in which, as far as I found, fungal exchange has been measured across several life stages (Hoysted et al. 2021 call earlier work single-stage snapshots) and in which only Mucoromycotina sequences have been found.

- **Can transfer:**
  - A stage-dependent payment rule: no detected carbon allocation to the fungus at the juvenile stage (n = 5), allocation at mature and retreating stages (n = 24 and 16), from *L. inundata* at Thursley, 2017 (Hoysted et al. 2021), as a hypothesis because stage covaries with season and tissue.
  - Pre-root nutrient uptake: 33P reached rhizoid-bearing juveniles with no rootlet, 15N did not (n = 5 and 6; Hoysted et al. 2021).
  - Orders of magnitude, tracer by tracer, from microcosms with mature plants (Hoysted et al. 2019; n = 20 for carbon, 10 for nutrients): 0.28% of fixed carbon allocated to the fungus over a labelling pulse of about 16 hours, and 3-9% of supplied 33P and 0.6-1% of supplied 15N taken up over 21 days. The windows differ, so the fractions are not a return per unit carbon and are not annual budgets.
  - A seasonal prevalence of root colonisation in a temperate, seasonally flooded habitat (14% in spring and 86% in autumn; 1,305 roots, 11 heaths; Kowal et al. 2020), not a flux multiplier.
  - Gametophyte form for this species: green, upright and lobed, with the fungus in the rhizoid base, overwintering in some individuals (Bruce 1972, Michigan).
  - A qualitative contrast in embryo support, months versus years, between green-gametophyte *Lycopodiella* and underground-gametophyte *Lycopodium* (Renzaglia and Whittier 2013, discussion-level).
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Any gametophyte nutrition number: none was measured. Do not give a gametophyte a carbon or nutrient budget from this card, and do not tag its mode as mycoheterotrophic until it is measured.
  - Partner identity for Rhynie plants: the fungi here are several modern Mucoromycotina FRE sequence types in a lineage with a Mesozoic or younger radiation, found in roots; Rhynie hosts were rootless and carried Glomeromycotina-like and Mucoromycotina-like fungi. Exclusive detection of one fungal group does not mean one fungal species.
  - Root-borne rates for mature and retreating plants to rootless axes: those plants take up through roots with long root hairs. Only the juvenile contrast bears on pre-root hosts.
  - A pure stage effect: stage is confounded with season, size, photosynthetic tissue and colonisation anatomy.
  - Absolute rates under Devonian conditions: the tracer fractions come from heath plants in modern CO2 and from short labelling windows.
  - Timing of independence: no days-to-independence figure for this species appeared in the sources read, and the matrotrophy durations are not species measurements.
  - Age of stage-dependent trade: a crown date does not date the origin of the trait. The experiments neither show nor exclude that it is inherited from older lycophytes, so this species is no evidence for or against a Devonian origin.

## Open questions

Goebel (1887, Botanische Zeitung 45: 161-190) for the first-hand gametophyte description, and a direct test of whether the Thursley gametophytes are green; carbon and nutrient exchange of the gametophyte, in the field; whether juvenile sporophytes draw carbon from the gametophyte or fungal necromass; whether mature plants feed gametophytes through a shared network (suggested, not shown); spore size and viability; any Cenozoic Lycopodiella-type spore record (the audit points to a middle-Miocene palynological report that I could not read, and which would not by itself be assignable to the living species); whether the seasonal colonisation pattern survives in sites with different hydrology; and the published New Phytologist version's differences from the accepted manuscript.

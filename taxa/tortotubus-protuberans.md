## In brief

Tortotubus protuberans is a microscopic, filamentous organic fossil found in dispersed palynological residues from the latest Ordovician or earliest Silurian into the Early Devonian. Smith (2016) showed that two previously separate fossil types are growth stages of one organism that builds cords of intertwined filaments, and argued it is a fungus with dikaryan features. Press coverage called it the oldest terrestrial fossil and oldest fungus; Smith himself cautioned it need not be the first organism on land. If fungal, it is among the earliest evidence for decomposers and nutrient-translocating mycelium before vascular plants were common.

## Morphology and anatomy

Observed (Smith 2016 abstract): the early filamentous stage, formerly Ornatifilum lornensis, has simple septal perforations and a two-layered cell wall. Bundles of intertwined filaments, enclosed by an elaborately sculptured surface, formed as secondary branches grew back along the main filament and then multiplied. Flask-shaped lateral cells once read as spore-bearing structures are broken filament bases (Berbee et al. 2020). The filaments are surface-ornamented; a secondary summary gives tubes about 10 µm wide and possible spores of about 25 µm at some branch tips. I could not read the methods of Smith's paper, so sizes are provisional.

## Age and distribution

Described by Johnson (1985) from the Early Silurian Tuscarora Formation of Pennsylvania, and recorded across northern Gondwana (Libya, Chad, and according to the abstract Oman and Saudi Arabia) and Laurentia (Thusu et al. 2014). Smith's material comes from Ludfordian rocks on Gotland, latest Silurian Old Red Sandstone on Kerrera and Early Devonian rocks near Albany, New York. Stage-level dating is by palynology, not radiometric, and the earliest stage is not firm (see range notes).

## Affinity and controversies

- Ascomycete: Sherwood-Pike & Gray (1985) read the flask-shaped appendages as spore-producing structures; Berbee et al. (2020) note that Smith's collections show these are broken filament bases, which undercuts that case.
- Dikarya: Smith (2016) argues the septal pores, bilayered wall and cord-forming growth indicate higher fungi.
- Not necessarily a fungus: Berbee et al. (2020) rate it possibly fungal but unlike any living taxon, leaving a protist or algal affinity open. The Artful Amoeba blog (2016) summarises Smith as excluding red algae on surface decoration, branching and pore size, but notes that many unrelated fungal groups grow similarly.
- Nematophyte or land-plant debris: Thusu et al. (2014) suggest the tubes are derived from nematophytes.

Evidence that would help: ultrastructure of the septal pores, chemistry (chitin versus other wall polymers) and attached reproductive structures.

## Ecology and physiology

If a fungus, a saprotroph or osmotroph. Cords, in living fungi, move and store nutrients between patches; Smith's framing is that this indicates sophisticated foraging in early land ecosystems (University of Cambridge 2016). What it decomposed is an open question: before vascular plants were common the candidates are microbial mats and soil crusts (Berbee et al. 2020 note coalified mat remains at about 449 Ma) and aeroterrestrial algae (Wellman & Strother 2015). Habitat is nearshore, either terrestrial (Smith) or marginal marine with land-derived input (Thusu et al.).

## Why it matters for modelling

- Timing: filament fossils that look like cord-forming mycelium appear at the Ordovician-Silurian boundary or in the Llandovery, some 30 Myr before the Rhynie plants, so a decomposer and nutrient-translocation compartment may predate vascular plant litter. Treat this as a low-confidence bound because of the dating uncertainty.
- Substrate: the organic matter available to such a fungus was non-vascular (mats, crusts, algae), which limits plausible decomposition rates and supports a patchy, slow detrital cycle; no rate data exist.
- Trophic base: later Lochkovian coprolites show animals digesting fungal mats and hyphae (Edwards et al. 2020), so a mycelial resource was available to detritivores by the Early Devonian.
- Biogeography and sampling: the record is palynological and nearshore, so absence from inland strata is not informative.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Contrast a pre-vascular decomposer and nutrient-translocation scenario with its absence, using cord-forming filaments whose fungal affinity and earliest occurrence remain uncertain.

- **Can transfer:**
  - T. protuberans, Smith's Gotland, Kerrera and Albany material: secondary branches growing back along main filaments and multiplying into intertwined cords supply a qualitative growth rule (Smith 2016, abstract).
  - T. protuberans, Smith's examined material: simple septal perforations and a two-layered wall characterise the early filamentous stage formerly called Ornatifilum lornensis (Smith 2016, abstract).
  - T. protuberans, Tuscarora Formation type material in Pennsylvania: an Early Silurian occurrence can anchor a conservative fossil-presence scenario without accepting the disputed Ordovician extension (Johnson 1985).
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not assign decomposition, nutrient uptake, storage or translocation rates from cord morphology; this card contains no rate data.
  - Do not encode fungal identity, dikaryan physiology or saprotrophy as established properties; protist and algal affinities remain open.
  - Do not use the earliest occurrence as a precise Ordovician–Silurian calibration: stage-level dating is palynological and uncertain.
  - Do not use the secondary tube and possible-spore dimensions as verified measurements; the primary methods were unavailable to this card's author.
  - Do not model flask-shaped lateral cells as reproductive organs or derive fecundity from them; they were reinterpreted as broken filament bases.

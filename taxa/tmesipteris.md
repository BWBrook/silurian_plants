## In brief

*Tmesipteris*, the fork ferns, is the sister genus of *Psilotum* in the Psilotaceae: about 15 species, mostly epiphytes on tree-fern trunks in Australasia and the south-west Pacific, with pendent axes bearing single-veined leaf-like appendages and bilobed synangia. It holds the largest genome known for any organism (160.45 Gbp per 1C in *T. oblanceolata*; Fernandez et al. 2024), and its gametophyte is a subterranean, vascularised, fungus-fed body that can produce a sporophyte directly in culture. Like *Psilotum*, it was once read as close to rhyniophytes and is now placed among ferns; see [Psilotum nudum](psilotum-nudum.md) for the phylogenetic argument.

## Morphology and anatomy

Rhizomes are subterranean and branched, with rhizoids. Aerial stems hang, are flattened, and are unbranched or forked; they grow indefinitely or end in a large leaf. The leaves are scale-like near the base and flattened toward the tip, spiral or two-ranked, with one vein and unequal, wedge-shaped bases. Sporophylls are forked and carry bilobed synangia on the upper margin; spores are monolete (all from the New Zealand factsheet). In *T. elongata* the stems are 70-1400 mm long, leaves 8-35 mm (rarely 45 mm) by 2-9 mm, and synangia 2.5-6 by 1-2.5 mm. Both generations contain vessel-like elements with scalariform perforation plates and sieve cells; Duckett & Ligrone (2005) reported the first vascular elements in the haploid generation of *Tmesipteris*. Pant & Khare (1971) described the epidermis, cuticle and stomatal ontogeny of *T. tannensis*.

## Age and distribution

About 15 species (factsheet; Fernandez et al. 2024): New Zealand five, Australia seven (including Lord Howe and Norfolk Islands), New Caledonia four, Solomon Islands and Vanuatu three each, and single species in Fiji, Samoa, the Society Islands and the Marquesas; the genus reaches the Philippines and New Guinea. *T. tasmanica* (Late Eocene-Oligocene, Tasmania) implies a cool, humid forest setting for the Cethana deposit (Carpenter 1988). Species delimitation is still moving: Chinnock (1975) recognised four New Zealand species and the factsheet now lists five.

## Affinity and controversies

Placement as in *Psilotum*: ferns, with Psilotales sister to Ophioglossales (Nitta et al. 2022). Niklas & Crepet (2022) found that a morphology-only matrix could not separate Psilotaceae from lycophyte-zosterophyll fossils. Within the genus, species relationships are unknown (Fernandez et al. 2023). A whole-genome multiplication has been inferred before the *Psilotum*-*Tmesipteris* split and a second one in the ancestor of *Tmesipteris* (as relayed by Fernandez et al. 2023).

## Ecology and physiology

*T. elongata* is mainly an epiphyte on tree-fern trunks, also on logs, rarely on the ground, and is larger in *Collospermum* clumps (factsheet). In Tasmania *T. elongata* is uncommon and *T. obliqua* moderately common, both usually on *Dicksonia antarctica* (Tasmanian key; pages give no data). Whittier (2004) cultured gametophytes of *T. lanceolata*, mycorrhizal in nature, in axenic culture with minerals and 0.5% glucose: they did not become photosynthetic, and about 15% produced apogamous sporophytes with stems and microphylls, joined directly to the gametophyte without a foot, with gametangia confined to gametophyte tissue. The sporophyte arose terminally, which Whittier reads as conversion of the gametophyte apical meristem to a shoot meristem. Gametophyte endophytes are glomeromycotean (Duckett & Ligrone 2005).

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Measured genome sizes.** *T. tannensis* 1C = 73.19 Gbp (tetraploid, 2n = 208); *T. obliqua* 147.29 Gbp (reported as octoploid, 2n = 416; 150.61 pg in Hidalgo et al. 2017); *T. oblanceolata* subsp. *linearifolia* 160.45 Gbp, about 7% above the previous record, *Paris japonica* (Fernandez et al. 2023, 2024). Base number x = 52, with n = 104 counted in four New Zealand species (Brownsey & Lovis 1987). Nothing in the sources links this to cell size, stomatal size or growth; those are the measurements to make.
- **Gametophyte to sporophyte without a foot.** Apogamy in about 15% of cultured gametophytes shows a shoot meristem can arise from a gametophyte apex; relevant to F1 as a route that bypasses the matrotrophic foot. Whether the apogamous sporophytes were haploid was not stated in the abstract.
- **Leaf-like appendages.** Single-veined, 8-35 mm appendages on a fern, nested far from lycophytes on current phylogenies, are a living case of microphyll-like organs outside the lycophytes, so the label "microphyll" alone carries no homology.
- **Limits of the analogy.** Pendent epiphytic axes on tree-fern trunks are not an analogue for erect Devonian axes (no support limit, and a substrate that was probably absent in the window; my inference), and I found no gas-exchange or hydraulic measurements for *Tmesipteris* itself; the *Psilotum* values in [Psilotum nudum](psilotum-nudum.md) cannot be assumed to transfer.
- **Measurements that could be made in Tasmania.** Gas exchange and light response, leaf area per axis and vein density, stomatal density and size, spore number per synangium, and excavation of gametophytes on *Dicksonia* trunks to time the spore-to-sporophyte interval.

## Open questions

Is the 160 Gbp genome a real upper limit, and does it constrain cell size or growth? How long does the gametophyte phase last in nature, and what carbon sources feed it? Are apogamous sporophytes haploid? What do the species relationships and the leaf homology look like in a proper phylogeny? Is there more than one Palaeogene macrofossil?

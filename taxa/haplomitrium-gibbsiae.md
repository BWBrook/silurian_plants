## In brief

*Haplomitrium gibbsiae* is a small, leafy, dioecious liverwort of fresh mineral soil in New Zealand and, rarely, Tasmania, in the earliest-diverging liverwort class. It has no rhizoids; its underground axes are colonised by Mucoromycotina fungi, and in isotope experiments that fungus delivered 15N and 33P to the plant at both 440 ppm and a simulated Palaeozoic 1500 ppm CO2 while the mycelium in the mesh cores took under 2% of fixed carbon in the 2019 experiment, a lower bound on the fungus's total take (Field et al. 2015, 2019). It is the fungal-nutrition exemplar: it is a living liverwort whose fungal partner belongs to the same subphylum as the Mucoromycotina-like endophytes reported from Rhynie plants (see [early arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi](early-glomeromycotan-fungi.md)), and it contrasts with the non-mycorrhizal [Marchantia polymorpha](marchantia-polymorpha.md) and with the Glomeromycotina-only *M. paleacea*.

## Morphology and anatomy

Gametophyte (Engel & Glenny 2008): leafy shoots arise from a prostrate, irregularly branched, colourless rhizome with a thick mucilage sheath produced by slime papillae; stems are unbranched above and reach 16-25(-30) mm; leaves are bright yellow-green, three-ranked and subequal, mostly one cell thick, with cells 40-65 micrometres wide and 45-85 micrometres long and 15-40 hyaline oil bodies per cell. Dioecious. After fertilisation a fleshy shoot-calyptra up to 8 mm high or more forms; the sporophyte is usually single, with a seta to 20-25 mm, a capsule about 0.5 x 3-4 mm and spores 23-29 micrometres across that cohere in tetrads or diads until maturity.

Below ground (Field et al. 2015, 2019; Carafa et al. 2003). The underground axes are enveloped in copious mucilage and lack rhizoids. Fungal colonisation is restricted to epidermal cells and strictly intracellular: fine hyphae 0.5-1.5 micrometres across form coils with swellings, called lumps, and thick-walled fungal structures lie in the mucilage at 440 ppm (interpreted as spores by Carafa et al.). Over half of the plant's biomass is these subterranean fungus-containing axes, which have a cylinder of food-conducting cells around central water-conducting cells (Field et al. 2015, citing Ligrone et al. 2012). The conducting cells are of the perforate type, not hydroids (Ligrone et al. 2012). Marchantia paleacea in the same experiment had coarse hyphae over 3 micrometres, arbuscules and large vesicles, and *Neohodgsonia mirabilis* showed both fine and coarse hyphae (Field et al. 2019). Carafa et al. (2003) called the Haplomitrium fungus arbuscule-forming and glomeromycotean-like; molecular work later placed it in Mucoromycotina (see below).

## Age and distribution

Living: New Zealand, widespread but rarely abundant, and Tasmania, where it is rare (Engel & Glenny 2008); also Chile and India under the broad circumscription, and Guangxi, China, a first East Asian record (Han et al. 2025). The fungal survey that identified its partner drew mainly on Tasmanian and New Zealand Gondwanan sites (Bidartondo et al. 2011). Fossil record and divergence estimates are in the range notes: no fossil in the window in the sources read, a Permian fossil genus reported second-hand, and an early-Devonian divergence from other liverworts from an older clock.

## Affinity and controversies

Position. Haplomitriopsida, with *Haplomitrium* and *Treubia*, is the earliest-diverging liverwort lineage (Field et al. 2015; Ligrone et al. 2012). Bidartondo et al. (2011) describe it as the earliest-diverging clade of land plants, a liverwort-first reading, while Field et al. (2015) call bryophytes the most basal extant land plants with their internal order still debated. Recent phylogenomics instead recovers monophyletic bryophytes, with bryophytes and tracheophytes both derived from a more complex ancestor and bryophytes having lost vasculature and stomata genes (Harris et al. 2020, 2022). Ligrone et al. (2012) infer that the last common ancestor of land plants probably had Haplomitrium-like perforate water-conducting cells restricted to the gametophyte; under the Harris reading that condition could be reduced or derived instead. This card treats Haplomitrium as basal within liverworts only.

Which fungus. Carafa et al. (2003) described the Haplomitrium association as arbuscule-bearing and glomeromycotean-like, and the Engel & Glenny (2008) account still lists the fungal partner as a glomeromycote. Bidartondo et al. (2011), with primers that detect Mucoromycotina, found Endogone-like Mucoromycotina in at least six Haplomitriopsida species rather than Glomeromycota, and Field et al. (2015, 2019) confirmed Mucoromycotina in *H. gibbsiae* from wild plants and at the end of experiments. Field et al. (2019) note that most arbuscular-mycorrhiza primers do not amplify Mucoromycotina DNA, so older nutritional attributions to Glomeromycotina may have involved both groups.

Circumscription. Han et al. (2025) record that the species was synonymised with South American *H. chilense* and that Indian taxa were subsumed in it, and that Engel & Glenny (2008) proposed 15 species in the genus where most revisions accept seven; the dimensions and habitat in this card are the New Zealand description.

Ancestral or derived. Field et al. (2015) allow that the Mucoromycotina symbiosis could be a derived trait by convergence or reversion, and judge this unlikely because Devonian fossils carry such fungi and the living associations are mutualistic. They also caution that responses of extinct early plant-fungus symbioses are unknown, so the experiments measure phenotypic plasticity in extant species.

## Ecology and physiology

Why the symbiosis is probably important to this plant (inference): with no rhizoids at all, *H. gibbsiae* is likely to be highly dependent on its fungus for phosphorus and other soil nutrients (Field et al. 2015); the tracer data show that the fungus delivers nutrients, not that it is the only route. Axenic *H. gibbsiae* failed to produce the naked, mucilage-invested underground axes, and re-inoculation with isolated fungus restored them within 3 months, with colonisation again confined to epidermal cells (Field et al. 2015). The axenic plants were maintained for over 2 years as green aerial tissue on agar, so the fungus is needed for the underground axes, not shown to be needed for survival in culture. The axenic fungal isolates described in detail came from *Treubia lacunosa*, and I did not resolve which host the re-inoculating isolate came from.

Experiment 1 (Field et al. 2015). Wild stock of *H. gibbsiae* and *Treubia lacunosa* from the South Island of New Zealand (April 2012) was potted in acid-washed sand with 5% moss-peat and native soil, with mesh-walled cores of 10 micrometres to admit hyphae but exclude rhizoids. Plants acclimated for 12 weeks at 15 °C day and 12 °C night, 12 h, 50 micromol photons per m2 per s and 70% relative humidity, at 440 ppm or 1500 ppm CO2 (n = 10 plants per CO2 level in Methods; the Table 1 legend gives n = 10 and 5; the 1500 ppm treatment simulates the mid-Palaeozoic and 440 ppm a near-contemporary atmosphere). Then 33P orthophosphate and 15N ammonium chloride were added to the cores, fungal connections were cut in controls by rotating cores, and plant-to-fungus carbon was traced with a 14CO2 pulse 21 days after the nutrient labels.
- Under both CO2 treatments, *H. gibbsiae* gained 33P and 15N through its fungus, which makes the association a two-way exchange of carbon for nutrients.
- The share of fixed carbon given to the fungus did not differ with CO2 in *H. gibbsiae*, whereas *T. lacunosa* gave a larger share at 1500 ppm; absolute carbon delivered to the mesh cores rose at 1500 ppm in both.
- Plants did not receive proportionally more nutrient at 1500 ppm. P-for-carbon efficiency showed no significant CO2 effect, with a trend to lower efficiency at 1500 ppm, and N-for-carbon efficiency no significant effect, with a trend to higher efficiency at 440 ppm. With these sample sizes, no significant effect is not demonstrated invariance.
- Against *Marchantia paleacea* and *Preissia quadrata* with Glomeromycotina, grown under the same conditions in an earlier experiment (Field et al. 2012), the authors report much greater P-for-carbon efficiency for the Mucoromycotina associations, especially at 440 ppm, whereas the Glomeromycotina efficiency fell dramatically from 1500 to 440 ppm. That comparison crosses experiments; Field et al. (2012) is read here by abstract only, and the fungal card holds its magnitudes. Mean values are in Figs 3-5 of Field et al. (2015), not in the text.

Experiment 2 (Field et al. 2019). Three liverworts, each with a known fungal partner, grown in non-sterile soil with native microbes in the same cabinets: *H. gibbsiae* with Mucoromycotina only (South Island, December 2013), *Marchantia paleacea* with Glomeromycotina only (Veracruz, Mexico, cloud forest, propagated by gemmae), and *Neohodgsonia mirabilis* with both (South Island). Forty microcosms per species, 20 at each of 440 and 1500 ppm, 12 weeks of acclimation, 5 replicate pots per treatment combination, chamber settings as given in the Methods (15/12 °C, 12 h, 50 micromol photons per m2 per s, 70% relative humidity). One core per pot then received 33P (223 ng) and, in half the pots, 15N-labelled Chlorella necromass (0.05 g algae, 2800 micrograms 15N per pot), with rotated cores as controls; a 14CO2 pulse (1.1 MBq) followed 21 days later. Figures 3-5 label bars by symbiont type; because each type is one species here, the 'Mucoromycotina' bars are presumably *H. gibbsiae* (my inference), although the text states the ratios jointly for the *Haplomitrium* and *Neohodgsonia* partnerships and gives no separate or combined mean. Fungal biomass was not quantified.
- Nitrogen: only plants whose fungi could reach the algae gained significant 15N. Plants with Mucoromycotina, singly or in dual symbiosis (*Haplomitrium* and *Neohodgsonia*), had mean 15N contents over 2.4 times those of Glomeromycotina-only *M. paleacea* at 440 ppm and over 90 times at 1500 ppm (the text assigns Fig. 3a to 440 ppm, whereas the legend assigns it to 1500 ppm), and 15N concentrations 35-90 times higher. CO2 had no significant effect. The route is not shown to be direct decomposition: the authors list better competition with saprotrophs, uptake of microbially mineralised N and a supportive mycorrhizosphere microbial community as alternatives.
- Phosphorus: both fungi raised 33P uptake. At 1500 ppm total 33P uptake was nearly twice as high in *M. paleacea* as in the Mucoromycotina-associated plants; at 440 ppm there was no significant difference. 33P concentration was highest in *M. paleacea*, with a symbiont by CO2 interaction.
- Carbon: the 14C reaching external mycelium in the mesh cores was under 2% of fixed carbon in all three symbioses; this is core mycelium only, not total allocation to fungal tissue. Dual symbiosis received a larger share, and over 5 times the total 14C of the single-fungus plants; total allocation was about 4 times higher at 1500 ppm than at 440 ppm across symbioses and rose with algae present.
- The authors' summary: Mucoromycotina-only gave substantial nitrogen access and increased phosphorus return for relatively little carbon, especially at modern CO2; Glomeromycotina-only gave very effective phosphorus transfer with no significant nitrogen from the organic patch; dual symbiosis combined both at considerably higher carbon cost. The text's Results credit the biomass difference to a 'Mucoromycotina-only (*Neohodgsonia*)' plant, which contradicts the Methods; I did not use the biomass result.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Two nutrient channels and a carbon price.** Fungal nutrition can enter a model as a host-by-fungus trait with channel-specific returns: nitrogen from organic matter via Mucoromycotina, phosphorus via either fungus (Glomeromycotina often more effective in total uptake), and a carbon cost that is low for single-fungus and high for dual associations, measured as carbon reaching core mycelium (Field et al. 2019).
- **CO2 sensitivity differs by fungus.** In these liverworts, Glomeromycotina phosphorus-for-carbon efficiency fell as CO2 dropped from 1500 to 440 ppm (Field et al. 2012, abstract), whereas Mucoromycotina efficiency in Haplomitriopsida showed no significant change (Field et al. 2015). A Palaeozoic CO2 fall therefore need not penalise a Mucoromycotina-hosting rootless plant in the way it penalised the Glomeromycotina-hosting thalloid liverworts. The direction is not uniform: in the dual-symbiosis liverworts *Allisonia* and *Neohodgsonia*, Field et al. (2016, abstract only) report higher P-for-carbon and N-for-carbon efficiency in the Mucoromycotina association at 440 than at 1500 ppm, a stronger response than the non-significant trend in Haplomitriopsida.
- **Dependence without roots.** A rhizoid-less plant with fungus-induced underground axes and its own conducting cells (see Morphology) is an example of a rootless plant that probably relies heavily on a symbiont for soil nutrients. The Rhynie hosts are also rootless and carried Mucoromycotina-like fungi alongside Glomeromycotina-like ones (Field et al. 2015, citing Strullu-Derrien et al. 2014); see [Horneophyton](horneophyton-lignieri.md) and [Nothia](nothia-aphylla.md).
- **Counterpoint to Marchantia polymorpha.** *M. polymorpha* subsp. *ruderalis* forms no mycorrhizas and the loss is recent (see its card), though it hosts diverse non-mycorrhizal fungal endophytes, some of which promote growth (Bowman et al. 2022); the pair therefore contrasts mycorrhizal dependence, not the presence of fungi. The experimental *Marchantia* was *M. paleacea*, a different species.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Fungal nutrition, set against the non-mycorrhizal [Marchantia polymorpha](marchantia-polymorpha.md) as a conceptual comparison, not an experimentally matched control, through a rhizoid-less liverwort that probably depends heavily on Mucoromycotina fungi for soil nutrients, measured with tracers at modern and simulated Palaeozoic CO2.

- **Can transfer:**
  - *H. gibbsiae* (wild stock, South Island NZ, April 2012; microcosms with 12 weeks of acclimation at 50 micromol per m2 per s and 15/12 °C, then 21 days of nutrient tracing before the 14CO2 pulse): the Mucoromycotina association is a reciprocal carbon-for-nutrient exchange; the fungus delivered 33P and 15N (ammonium) under both 440 and 1500 ppm, the share of fixed carbon given to the fungus did not differ significantly with CO2, and efficiency per carbon showed no significant CO2 effect (Field et al. 2015).
  - *H. gibbsiae* with its Mucoromycotina, against *M. paleacea* with Glomeromycotina (wild *H. gibbsiae* stock from the South Island, December 2013; gemma-propagated *M. paleacea* from Mexico; 5 pots per treatment): Mucoromycotina-hosting plants gained far more 15N from algal necromass (over 2.4-fold at 440 ppm to over 90-fold at 1500 ppm, stated jointly for the *Haplomitrium* and *Neohodgsonia* partnerships) while *M. paleacea* gained about twice as much 33P at 1500 ppm, with no significant difference at 440 ppm; under 2% of fixed carbon reached core mycelium in all plants (Field et al. 2019). Use as relative channel strengths, not as species-specific coefficients.
  - *H. gibbsiae* axenic and re-inoculated: the underground axes form only with the fungus; use as a morphological dependence of host development on the symbiont (Field et al. 2015, in vitro).
  - *H. gibbsiae* size, spore and sporophyte dimensions from the New Zealand Flora (stems up to 1.6-2.5(-3.0) cm; seta 20-25 mm; capsule 0.5 x 3-4 mm; spores 23-29 micrometres); these describe the species generally, not the isotope specimens.
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not use the Field et al. (2015) and (2019) fluxes as field or Palaeozoic rates: microcosms at 50 micromol per m2 per s, 15/12 °C and 70% humidity, with CO2 at two levels only, 1500 ppm taken from geochemical carbon-cycle models rather than a time series.
  - Do not attribute the *H. gibbsiae* versus *M. paleacea* differences to the fungus alone: plant lineage, continent of origin, growth form and fungus are confounded in a three-species design, and the 2015 comparison with *M. paleacea* and *Preissia quadrata* is against data from an earlier experiment.
  - Do not read per-species *H. gibbsiae* numbers from the joint statements: the quoted fold-changes cover the *Haplomitrium* and *Neohodgsonia* partnerships together, and per-species means are only in the figures.
  - Do not turn the carbon figures into total fungal cost: they cover core mycelium only, and a model needs a separate allowance for fungal carbon elsewhere.
  - Do not convert nutrient delivery into an efficiency per unit fungal biomass: fungal biomass was not measured.
  - Do not read the 15N result as direct fungal decomposition of organic nitrogen: microbial mineralisation and competition are alternative routes.
  - Do not treat non-significant CO2 effects (2015) as demonstrated CO2 invariance, or the underground-axis dependence as a need for the fungus for survival.
  - Do not treat ammonium (2015) and algal necromass (2019) as interchangeable nitrogen sources.
  - Do not transfer the efficiency values to *M. polymorpha*: the model liverwort is non-mycorrhizal, and *M. paleacea* is a different species (see the Marchantia card).
  - Do not assume the Rhynie fungi traded nutrients as these liverworts do: the fossils show fungal affinity and anatomy, and exchange in them is inferred, never measured (fungal card).
  - Do not treat the Haplomitriopsida condition as ancestral for embryophytes, or its conducting cells as hydroids or xylem.
  - Do not apply the New Zealand description to all material called *H. gibbsiae*: the species is circumscribed broadly (Han et al. 2025).

## Open questions

Per-species means for *H. gibbsiae* from the figures or the data deposit; whether the Mucoromycotina of Haplomitrium are facultative saprotrophs in nature and which nitrogen forms they pass on; where in the lumps and coils the exchange occurs; whether Harris et al.'s crown-liverwort dating (440-412 Ma) sampled Haplomitrium, and any Haplomitriopsida fossil older than the Late Permian Gessella; and how much of the Rhynie plants' soil access Mucoromycotina-like fungi supplied.

## In brief

Archaeopteris is the Late Devonian forest tree: the first plant to combine a woody trunk (bifacial cambium, secondary xylem and phloem), perennial branches carrying deciduous leafy shoots, endogenous roots and heterospory. It was presented as "the earliest known modern tree" (Meyer-Berthaud et al. 1999). Archaeopteris-type roots in the mid-Givetian Cairo palaeosol (about 385 Ma) reach radial extents of up to 11 m, with primary roots 6-16 cm wide (Stein et al. 2020). In the height race it is where the trajectory that starts with 1-6 cm [Cooksonia](cooksonia.md) arrives: inferred trees of 11-32 m, though every height here is model-dependent.

## Morphology and anatomy

Until 1960 the leafy, heterosporous remains were read as fern fronds; their connection to axes with conifer-like wood (Callixylon) changed that (Meyer-Berthaud & Decombeix 2009), and Beck (1962) argued for a progymnosperm. Trunk and proximal branches are perennial; ultimate and penultimate branches are deciduous, so growth is three-dimensional, not mainly vertical as in cladoxylopsids (Meyer-Berthaud & Decombeix 2009). A 5 m trunk of a young Moroccan tree (C. erianum) shows short-lived apical branches (type A), persistent adventitious branches (type B) and latent primordia (type H) that could become roots or shoots, plausibly after burial by overbank sediment (Meyer-Berthaud et al. 2000). Leaves are small, laminate and fan-shaped with open dichotomous venation, present from the Givetian (Tomescu & Whitewoods 2024); in A. roemeriana they are anisophyllous on dorsiventral branches (Fairon-Demaret & Leponce 2001).

Wood is eustelic with mesarch primary xylem and pitted secondary tracheids (Tanrattana et al. 2017), doing both conduction and support. Cell walls fill 31% of one Callixylon section, against 85% in the cladoxylopsid Xinicaulis from the same locality (Xu et al. 2017; single sections). Roots are endogenous and secondary-thickened, 25-90 mm wide in Moroccan material (Meyer-Berthaud et al. 2013). I found no ring counts, growth rates or wood densities.

## Age and distribution

Cairo gives the oldest evidence of the large root systems, 2-3 Myr older than Gilboa; archaeopteridaleans are among the plant groups collected there (Stein et al. 2020). Fronds appear in the mid to late Givetian, the microspore near the base of the stage (Berry 2019). Early Frasnian records are small in situ tree bases in Svalbard and drifted fronds at South Mountain, New York (Berry 2019). Famennian records span Belgium, the Donets Basin, Ireland, Morocco and Pennsylvania, with a trunk base at about 70 degrees S in South Africa (Gess & Berry 2024); the genus grew inside the palaeoantarctic circle and on the palaeoequator (Capel et al. 2022). The project's PBDB extract (485-383 Ma) holds only two Givetian Archaeopteris occurrences (United States, Belgium): a window artefact.

## Affinity and controversies

Lignophyte placement is secure and a relationship to seed plants is likely (Stein et al. 2020). Genus integrity is not: the group "shows significant variation" and is probably taxonomically diverse. For the Middle Devonian, Berry (2019) asks whether trunks carried large long-lived boughs or only attached short-lived fronds, and the Cairo roots are assigned because they match features presumed for Archaeopteris and no other known Middle Devonian plant. Dominance timing is contested. The textbook chain from forests to CO2 drawdown, anoxia and extinction (Algeo & Scheckler 1998) meets East Greenland data in which Archaeopteris became dominant during and after the Kellwasser carbon-isotope excursions, not before, with volcanism the proposed cause (Marshall et al. 2025; one section).

## Ecology and physiology

The Cairo vertisol records seasonal wetting and drying; Stein et al. infer that a stable soil was needed for tree size and dominance, and read the roots as probably perennial structural roots plus ephemeral feeder rootlets, as in modern woody trees. Famennian Catskill charcoal sits beside abundant Archaeopteris, but mostly Rhacophyton burned (Cressler 2001; then called the oldest charcoal, but charcoal is now known from the Silurian, Glasspool et al. 2004). A. roemeriana sporangia average 1.94 mm by 0.62 mm, 39 on the one fertile leaf counted in full; microspores are under 70 um, megaspores 110-403 um (Fairon-Demaret et al. 2001). Gametophytes are unknown.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Size anchor (F2).** Cooksonia 1-6 cm at about 432 Ma; [Pertica](pertica.md) perhaps 1-3 m; [Wattieza](wattieza.md) at least 8 m by the early Frasnian; Archaeopteris 11-32 m at about 385 Ma. Cooksonia to 8 m is a 130-800-fold height gain in about 50 Myr, a doubling every 5-7 Myr (my arithmetic on single-taxon maxima).
- **Root scaling.** Cairo regressions (18 trees): trunk base diameter = 3.19 x largest root diameter - 6.4 cm (RMA); rooting depth rises with tree size, Eospermatopteris depth does not. Roots taper from 6-7 cm at 2 m to 2.5-3.5 cm at 8 m; laterals 1-1.5 cm at 4-6 m; rootlets 1-2 mm to at least 10 m (Stein et al. 2020).
- **Soil volume.** Radius 11 m by depth 1.2-1.6 m is 450-600 m3, against under 4 m3 for Eospermatopteris (radius 1-2 m, depth to 30 cm): about 10^2-fold (my arithmetic, upper bound).
- **Life history.** Perennial trunk, deciduous shoots, indeterminate secondary growth, resprouting from latent primordia after burial.
- **Dispersal (F4).** Wind-dispersed microspores from 11-32 m crowns; heavier megaspores. Geminospora lemurata has long been tied to archaeopteridaleans, but a spore type shows presence, not abundance or forest cover (Cascales-Miñana et al. 2026).
- **Earth-system tests.** A 10 per mil lithium-isotope rise across 378-385 Ma is attributed to expanding deep-rooted plants (Liu et al. 2025); D'Antonio et al. (2022) note that root innovations precede the Hangenberg event by 25-30 Myr.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Contrast woody support, extensive rooting and differentiated reproductive structures with smaller early plant architectures, using preserved root and fertile-organ measurements while keeping tree heights model-dependent.

- **Can transfer:**
  - Archaeopteris-type trees, Cairo root-system impressions: use trunk-base diameters 8-45 cm across 18 trees, retaining the impressions' imprecision and comparative identification (Stein et al. 2020).
  - Archaeopteris-type Cairo root systems: represent 10-15 primary roots per tree, 6-16 cm wide, radial extent to 11 m and depth 1.2-1.6 m as site-specific architecture (Stein et al. 2020).
  - Archaeopterid roots, Famennian Anti-Atlas permineralised specimens: represent endogenous, secondary-thickened roots 25-90 mm wide (Meyer-Berthaud et al. 2013).
  - A. roemeriana, Upper Famennian Belgium: use mean sporangium dimensions 1.94 mm by 0.62 mm and 39 sporangia on the single fully counted fertile leaf (Fairon-Demaret et al. 2001).
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not treat Cairo tree heights as measured stature; they are regression estimates using generic-conifer allometry, with Araucaria used for root-diameter conversion.
  - Do not treat the Belgian megaspores as directly observed inside the assigned sporangia; their association is inferred from surrounding matrix.
  - Do not assign empirical growth rates, lifespans or wood densities from this card; these measurements were not found.
  - Do not treat post-burial resprouting as an observed recovery rate or demonstrated response; the Moroccan latent primordia support a plausible developmental interpretation.
  - Do not convert archaeopteridalean dispersed-spore occurrence into abundance or forest cover.

## Open questions

Is Archaeopteris monophyletic? Were Givetian trunks woody enough to support the Cairo root systems? What were growth rates and lifespans? Heights rest on a conifer allometry; would a lignophyte-specific one change them?

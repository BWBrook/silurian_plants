## In brief

Sawdonia ornata is the best-known spiny zosterophyll. Its axes carry multicellular spines, branch pseudomonopodially, end in coiled (circinate) tips and bear single laterally attached sporangia, each with a larger spiny outer valve and a smaller smooth inner one. Hueber (1971) gave the name Sawdonia ornata to the plant Dawson had described from Gaspé as Psilophyton princeps var. ornatum. It matters because in the latest cladistic analysis the Sawdoniaceae, with their emergences, circinate tips and elliptical xylem, are the closest zosterophyll relatives of the lycopsids (Claisse et al. 2025).

## Morphology and anatomy

The sporangia are the best-established feature. Gensel & Berry (2016), studying material from the type locality, found that each sporangium has unequal valves: a larger, more rounded outer (abaxial) valve into which the stalk merges imperceptibly and which is covered with spines, and a smaller, thinner, flat inner valve without spines. Sporangia lie near, and well below, the apex of aerial stems, on one or two sides, curving toward the stem at an acute angle. Lateral branching of the pseudomonopodial system varies in density and angle.

Some quantitative values from Hueber's diagnosis are available only through a secondary summary and need checking: stems up to 5 mm wide, spines 0.5-3.9 mm long with bases of 0.5-1.8 mm at 4-45 per cm of stem, sporangia 3-3.5 mm across, trilete spores 54-64 µm, an oval xylem strand and stomata on the stem but not on the spines. Scottish material, now treated as cf. Sawdonia sp., showed planar branching, abaxial emergences near branch points, H-branching, rosettes and papillae on the cuticle, and pyritised xylem with tracheid structure (Rayner 1983). Circinate tips are known in the genus (Berry & Gensel 2019) and are a synapomorphy of the Sawdoniaceae (Claisse et al. 2025).

## Age and distribution

The type locality is at Gaspé, in beds of Emsian age (Lalica & Tomescu 2021, for the formation). The species concept has narrowed. Gensel & Berry (2016) emended the genus, moved Sawdonia acanthotheca (Gensel et al. 1975, Middle Devonian, New Brunswick) to another genus, and treated some other records as cf. Sawdonia; Claisse et al. (2025) accordingly set aside the UK and Poland occurrences. A new, much younger species in the late Middle to early Late Devonian of Venezuela shows that the genus lasted about 20 Myr (Berry & Gensel 2019).

## Affinity and controversies

Sawdonia belongs among the zosterophylls, which in recent analyses form a grade paraphyletic to the lycopsids (Claisse et al. 2025). In that analysis Sawdonia groups with Oricilla and Tarella, inside the Sawdoniaceae that are sister to Asteroxylon plus Drepanophycus. Gensel et al. (1975) discussed spine-like emergences in relation to the origin of microphylls and lateral sporangia; whether emergences were microphyll precursors remains open in the sources read. Because the genus concept changed in 2016, older records of 'Sawdonia' need re-examination.

## Ecology and physiology

Circinate tips imply apical growth by unrolling, and sporangia positioned well below the apex suggest that fertile and growing regions coexisted (both inferences). The function of the spines is unresolved. If Hueber's observation that stomata are on stems and not spines holds, spines were unlikely to be gas-exchange surfaces. In the related Forania, stomata occur at the bases of large emergences (Jensen & Gensel 2013), and in Staphylophyton, an emergence-covered zosterophyll possibly closely related to Sawdonia, no undoubted stomata were observed (preserved or absent), so if they were absent carbon uptake might have been through thin cuticle, from underground organs, or by mycoheterotrophy or parasitism (Gensel et al. 2025). S. ornata is Emsian, the interval in which zosterophyll richness began to fall (Cascales-Miñana & Meyer-Berthaud 2014).

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Spines and surface area.** If spines were cones with the ranges above, their surface would range from about 1% of the stem surface to about three times it for a 5 mm stem (my cone calculation from secondary values; the extremes are unlikely to co-occur). Since stomata are reported only on the stem (secondary summary), a default model should give spines no gas-exchange role.
- **Growth rule.** Indeterminate, unrolling apical growth with a pseudomonopodial main axis and fertile lateral branches below the tip.
- **Reproduction.** Single sporangia 3-3.5 mm wide with unequal valves, and trilete spores of 54-64 µm, which are values to check.
- **Phylogenetic anchor.** The Sawdoniaceae-lycopsid sister relation lets a model treat Sawdonia as the best sampled proxy for the zosterophyll-to-lycopsid transition; all character values should be borrowed with that uncertainty.
- **Temporal pattern.** Zosterophyll cladogenesis continued through the Lochkovian and relented in the Pragian (Claisse et al. 2025), and richness declined from the Emsian, so S. ornata belongs to the declining phase of the group and the genus persisted long after it.

**Chosen to contrast:** Contrast lateral reproduction below the shoot apex with terminal-sporangium architectures, using Gaspé material with unequal valves while keeping proposed spine gas-exchange functions uncalibrated.

- **Can transfer:**

- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Do not use Hueber's numerical stem, spine, stomatal or spore values as verified parameter estimates; this card obtained them through an unchecked secondary summary.
  - Do not assign Scottish planar branching, H-branching or cuticular details directly to S. ornata; that material is now cf. Sawdonia sp.

## Open questions

Is the Scottish material S. ornata? What are the tracheid type, spore wall and stomatal structure of S. ornata, checked against Hueber (1971) and Rayner (1983)? Does Sawdonia have an identifiable rooting system? Did the spines serve defence, photosynthesis or neither? Does the Emsian type age hold up against spore data from Gaspé?

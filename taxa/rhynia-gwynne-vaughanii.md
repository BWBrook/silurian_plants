## In brief

Rhynia gwynne-vaughanii is the type "rhyniophyte" from the Rhynie chert: a leafless, branching, rootless plant about 20 cm tall with a small strand of S-type tracheids, terminal spindle-shaped sporangia and stomata over its axes. Unlike Aglaophyton it has genuine tracheids, so it anchors the stem-tracheophyte end of the Rhynie plants. It is unusual among early plants in that its life cycle is known: sporophyte, both sexes of gametophyte (Remyophyton) and asexual propagules that develop from epidermal cells.

## Morphology and anatomy

Observed in thin section: creeping axes bearing hemispherical projections with rhizoids, from which upright axes arise; branching is mostly by adventitious lateral axes, not dichotomy (D.S. Edwards 1980, in Kerp 2017). Kidston and Lang's original sporangia attributed to Rhynia belong to Aglaophyton; Rhynia's own attached sporangia are very rare, so its sporophytic status was proved only in 1980 (D.S. Edwards 1980), after decades in which Rhynia axes were read as gametophytes (Kerp 2017).

The strand is small relative to the stem. Tracheids carry broad annular or helical thickenings of S-type, a wall construction that combines tracheid and moss-hydroid features (Kenrick and Crane 1991). A zone of long unpitted parenchyma with oblique end walls surrounds it, in the phloem position, without sieve areas. Inner and outer cortex are differentiated, the outer only one or two cell layers thick; the hypodermis is one cell thick; the cuticle is up to 4 µm. Stomata are 90 to 100 × 75 to 85 µm and lack cutinised hypodermal channels, unlike Aglaophyton (Kerp 2017). Sporangia are up to 5.1 × 2.4 mm with a three-layered wall, an abscission zone and a sterile basal pad; spores are about 39 µm.

## Age and distribution

Rhynie chert, Aberdeenshire: hot-spring sinter in a half-graben on the Old Red Sandstone continent (Laurussia), c. 25 to 28° S (Wellman 2017; Edwards et al. 2017). The U-Pb age of the underlying Milton of Noth Andesite is 411.5 ± 1.3 Ma (Parry et al. 2011); the 40Ar/39Ar age of hydrothermal adularia is 407.6 ± 2.2 Ma (Mark et al. 2013), which Edwards et al. (2017) favour for the hot-spring activity. On the project timescale, Pragian and earliest Emsian respectively. Spore biostratigraphy gives early Pragian to ?earliest Emsian. Rhynia spores resemble those of other plants and cannot be traced beyond the Rhynie basin (Wellman 2017).

## Affinity and controversies

The S-type cell is shared with Stockmansella and Huvenia, and Kenrick and Crane (1991) recovered a Rhyniaceae clade as the basal clade of tracheophytes if S- and G-types are homologous; that rests on one character system. Gerrienne et al. (2006) proposed that Rhyniopsida, which would include Rhynia, differ from eutracheophytes in having more or less isomorphic generations; the hypothesis leans on a single Cooksonia specimen. The earlier idea that Rhynia was Aglaophyton's gametophyte is refuted.

## Ecology and physiology

Stomata with substomatal chambers and specialised tissue beneath them point to high water-use efficiency (Edwards 1998). Rhynia grew in thickets of upright stems on wet sinter and sand; gametophytes grew in shallow pools on the apron (Channing 2017). Propagules 0.34 to 2.3 mm long with stomata, rhizoids and a vascular strand develop from epidermal cells and hemispherical projections, with apical growth beginning after stomata form and before vascular tissue (Kearney et al. 2016, reported by Kerp 2017).

## Why it matters for modelling

- Hydraulics: a small S-type tracheid strand supplies a 20 cm plant whose stomata are not sunken and whose water-use efficiency is inferred to be high (Edwards 1998); pair with the site's CO2 (about 3100 ppm in the GEOCARBSULF run quoted by Wellman 2017).
- Reproduction: terminal sporangia 5.1 × 2.4 mm with 39 µm spores; asexual propagules from epidermal cells give a vegetative route that needs no gametophyte (Kearney et al. 2016).
- Gametophytes: 1.5 × 1.5 cm patch with over 200 individuals; 27 archegoniophores and at least 69 antheridiophores recognised (Taylor et al. 2005), a male-biased count whose cause is unknown.
- Disturbance: plants rapidly recolonised sinter and sand after floods; use as a stress-tolerant ruderal strategy rather than a climax species (Wellman 2017).

## Open questions

Is the Rhyniaceae position of S-type cells robust to new data? What did the sporophyte-gametophyte junction in Remyophyton show? Do the S-type walls of Rhynia and the vesicle-bearing walls of Aglaophyton share an ancestry (Kerp 2017)?

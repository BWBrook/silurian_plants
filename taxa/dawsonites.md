## In brief

*Dawsonites* is a form genus for sporangium-bearing branch systems of Early Devonian plants: slender, dichotomising or sympodial branches ending in terminal, paired, fusiform sporangia 3-6 mm long that hold trilete spores of about 45-60 µm. It exists because fertile compressions are common, vegetative axes are not attached to them, and the sporangia cannot be placed in a named *Psilophyton* species. It probably stands for the fertile organs of several trimerophyte-grade basal euphyllophytes (my inference; the parents are not known), and matters to a simulation as a warning against counting form-taxa as plants and as a record of the terminal fertile unit.

## Morphology and anatomy

Halle (1916) described the genus from Røragen, Norway, as sporangium-bearing branch systems dividing dichotomously, or with a sympodial main axis and bifurcating lateral branches; ultimate branches are slender and curved, with terminal narrowly obovoid or short fusiform capsules usually 3-5 mm long (diagnosis as quoted by the Plant Fossil Names registry; I did not read Halle's paper). Rayner (1995) describes Ballanucater sporangia as 4-6 mm long, pendulous, borne terminally on dichotomising axes, in pairs that are often twisted about each other, dehiscing longitudinally and containing trilete spores averaging about 60 µm, with a finely sculptured outer exine that tends to detach. Chaloner et al. (1978) found *D. arcuatus* sporangia in Oxfordshire borehole cores as pairs with opposed longitudinal slits and spiralised mechanical tissue in the wall, with *Apiculiretusispora*-type spores adhering (abstract; the paper was not available in full). All are compressions or fragments; no anatomy of the axes is known.

## Age and distribution

The records I could read are Lower Devonian, Pragian to Emsian, in Laurussia: Røragen (Norway, "Lower Devonian" only); the Senni Beds of Wales, where Croft & Lang (1942) recorded typical examples at Llanover; Ballanucater Farm and the Callander area, Scotland, assigned to the Emsian by palynology (Rayner 1995); and an Emsian Oxfordshire borehole (Chaloner et al. 1978). PBDB lists 18 occurrences from 15 collections: 10 in Belgium, 3 in France and 1 each in the UK (2), Germany, Canada and Australia, mostly from monographs of the 1930s to 1960s; their stage assignments are contributors' and not independent evidence. The Senni Beds correlate with the lower Cosheston Group, whose spore assemblages are late Lochkovian to Pragian (Wellman et al. 1998), so the first stage here is Pragian, conservatively. Rayner (1995) takes the presence of *Dawsonites* at Ballanucater as indicating the *Psilophyton* Zone (Banks 1980, not read).

## Affinity and controversies

The genus is an "artificial" fossil-taxon in the sense of Cleal & Thomas (2021), useful because of taphonomy even though it does not map onto a natural taxon, and Capel et al. (2023) excluded it, with *Hostinella* and *Psilophytites*, from their diversity analysis for that reason. Its relation to [Psilophyton](psilophyton.md) is a taxonomic question with unsettled answers. Rayner (1995) assigns clusters of fusiform sporangia to *Dawsonites*, although sometimes connected to extensive dichotomous axes, because they lack features that identify a *Psilophyton* species. Fertile units of *P. crenulatum* resemble them: sporangia 3-5 x 1-2 mm consistently twisted around each other, spores 48-102 µm (Doran 1980). Spores of four *Psilophyton* species resemble *Apiculiretusispora* and *Calamospora* (Gensel & White 1983), overlapping the *Apiculiretusispora* comparisons for *Dawsonites*. The registry lists a combination *Psilophyton arcuatum* (Schweitzer 1980). But Croft & Lang (1942) described, at Llanover, a spiny *Psilophyton princeps*-type plant with a fructification in organic connection that was "quite unlike *Dawsonites*", of which typical examples also occurred. So *Dawsonites* is not simply the fertile part of every *Psilophyton*; it may combine several parent species, genera or even trimerophyte-grade lineages. Which parents, and whether any are lignophyte-line euphyllophytes (as *Wilhowia* and *Kenrickia* may be, see the Psilophyton card), is untested.

## Ecology and physiology

Only inferences. Pendulous terminal paired sporangia, spores of about 45-60 µm and longitudinal dehiscence suggest release from a branched, probably upright plant (my inference). At Llanover the plant fossils occur in or near fluvial-channel sediments of an alluvial plain and probably came from a variety of habitats (Cleal & Thomas 1995), so the parent plants' own habitat is unknown. Spiralised mechanical tissue in the sporangium wall (Chaloner et al. 1978) suggests a controlled dehiscence mechanism.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Counting units.** *Dawsonites* and *Psilophyton* are fossil-taxa for parts of overlapping parent plants. Occurrence or diversity data for F3 should merge or down-weight form-taxa to avoid double counting; Capel et al. (2023) excluded it entirely.
- **Fertile unit.** Terminal paired sporangia 3-6 mm long and spores of about 45-60 µm define a fecundity unit. A derived estimate (my arithmetic): five to seven full dichotomies in the fertile units of *Psilophyton crenulatum* (Doran 1980) would give at most 2^5 to 2^7 = 32-128 terminal sporangia per fertile unit if every dichotomy were complete; real counts are probably lower. Parameter values need the species descriptions, not this card.
- **Spore morphology as a trait.** Trilete, 45-60 µm, laevigate to micropapillate spores that match *Apiculiretusispora*/*Punctatisporites* dispersed types link euphyllophyte-grade plants to the dispersed-spore record (F3, F4), though these types are not specific to one lineage.
- **Range for dispersal modelling.** The Pragian to Emsian record spans Norway, Britain, Belgium, France, Germany, Canada and possibly Australia, so the fertile-unit type is widespread on Laurussia and perhaps present in Gondwana.

## Open questions

- Which *Psilophyton* species (or other trimerophytes) produced each *Dawsonites* species, from attached material or spore wall ultrastructure (compare Gensel & White 1983)?
- Are the Victorian *D. subarcuatus* and the Fair Isle *D. roskiliensis* correctly assigned, and is any record Middle Devonian?
- What is the age of Halle's Røragen beds in modern stages?
- Does a Pragian record older than the Senni Beds exist?

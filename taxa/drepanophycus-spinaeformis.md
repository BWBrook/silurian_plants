## In brief

Drepanophycus spinaeformis is a long-ranging herbaceous lycopsid with microphylls and stem-borne sporangia, known mostly from Devonian compressions in Laurussia and China. Göppert described it in 1852 as a plant allied with the fucoid algae; the type was later lost and a neotype was designated only in 2000 (Li et al. 2000). The name became a catch-all for fragmentary lycopsid axes, so its stated range of tens of millions of years is probably a composite. Its congener D. qujingensis in Yunnan supplies the best quantified clonal rhizome system of any Early Devonian plant, with direct consequences for early soil stabilisation.

## Morphology and anatomy

The plants carried spirally arranged, drepanoid (curved) microphylls with leaf traces on stems that branched dichotomously or pseudomonopodially, and arose from rhizomes with H- or K-branching (Rayner 1984; Geng & Zhu 1994; Xue et al. 2016). Scottish Emsian specimens preserved as pyrite permineralisations give the xylem and tracheid structure, with perforated tracheid walls (Rayner 1984; Geng & Zhu 1994). Sporangia are the weak point. Rayner described kidney-shaped sporangia, Geng & Zhu found nearly spheroid sporangia on short stalks attached laterally to the axis near the leaf axils, and for D. qujingensis the sporangia are two-valved, borne on short stalks directly on the stem, unrelated to the leaves, and supplied by a vascular trace wider than a leaf trace (Li & Edwards 1995). Fertile material is rare, and the sporangia of D. spinaeformis itself remain conjectural (Li & Edwards 1995).

Quantitative data come from Yunnan: rhizome traces 5.9-18 mm wide (mean 10.5 mm, n = 90) and aerial axes averaging 15.9 mm (Xue et al. 2016). I have no verified height.

## Age and distribution

Records span the Early Devonian of Scotland, eastern Canada, New York State and Guizhou, and the species was described as of world-wide distribution in the Lower Devonian (Geng & Zhu 1994); Halle's Yunnan material is now assigned to D. qujingensis (Li & Edwards 1995). Stubblefield & Banks (1978) argued from cuticles that it lived in the Early, Middle and Late Devonian and is not a reliable index of Lower Devonian strata. That wide range should be treated cautiously: Middle Devonian material from Xinjiang was named D. minor (Xu et al. 2013) and later transferred, with D. devonicus, to Sengelia (Matsunaga & Tomescu 2017). The Yunnan Xujiachong Formation is interpreted as Pragian (about 411-408 Ma in Xue et al. 2016's terms), and spore data bracket it as early Pragian to possibly earliest Emsian.

## Affinity and controversies

Leaves, leaf traces, roots and xylem indicate lycopsid affinity, but Rayner (1984) questioned whether the sporangial position fits that group, and Geng & Zhu (1994) raised the Drepanophycales to a class, Drepanophycopsida, between zosterophylls and lycopsids. Hueber (1992) regarded Drepanophycus as perhaps the earliest recognisable lycopsid, in contrast to Baragwanathia. Claisse et al. (2025) place it with Asteroxylon as a lycopsid clade sister to the Sawdoniaceae. The stomata are disputed: Stubblefield & Banks (1978) reported paracytic stomata with paired guard cells, whereas Sun et al. (2005) re-read the cuticles as anomocytic, as in a living Lycopodium, which removes the claim that Drepanophycus had the oldest paracytic stomata. Matsunaga & Tomescu (2017) place several former Drepanophycus species in Sengelia, leaving a narrower genus.

## Ecology and physiology

For D. qujingensis, rhizomes with H-branching formed large clones that survived repeated sediment burial on well-drained floodplain soils in a seasonal wet-dry climate, and the plant colonised new alluvium immediately (Xue et al. 2016). The authors also suggest that traces up to about 1 m deep in the Emsian Battery Point Formation of Gaspé, previously read as deep roots, may be rhizomes of a long-lived, progressively buried plant, with Drepanophycus one candidate; the parent plant there is unknown.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Belowground structure.** Rhizome density was 800-1,300 transections per m² of bedding surface, equal to 0.8-1.3 km of rhizome per m³ of sediment, or 6.9-11.3% of sediment volume (Xue et al. 2016). Rhizome length density is at the lower end of the range for modern roots, and the predicted reduction in erosion is weak to modest. In experiments cited by the authors, banks with 16-18% root volume resisted erosion 20,000 times better than unvegetated banks, so rhizome volume here is below that range.
- **Disturbance regime.** Overbank aggradation of 10-40 mm per year would bury a 2 m active soil zone in 50-200 years, and clone lifespans of decades to centuries are plausible by analogy with Lycopodium and Pteridium (Xue et al. 2016, inference). Carbonate nodules imply 10,000-200,000 years of residence, so many clone generations occupied one profile.
- **Life history.** Clonal, flood-tolerant and an early colonist, like Sengelia (Matsunaga & Tomescu 2017), with sexual reproduction probably infrequent (inference). Isotope evidence indicates low soil productivity while the carbonates formed.
- **Shared body plan.** Root-bearing and rooting axes are common across Drepanophycales (Hetherington et al. 2021), so Asteroxylon-type data can be borrowed with caution.
- **Caveat.** Size and rhizome data are from the Chinese congener; broad stratigraphic range makes species-level time series unsafe.

## Open questions

Is D. spinaeformis a single species across the Devonian? What are its sporangium position and spores? Are its stomata anomocytic or paracytic? Do D. qujingensis data carry over? What plant made the Gaspé traces?

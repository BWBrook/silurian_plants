## In brief

Yarravia is a Victorian early land plant with naked, rarely branching axes that end in compact terminal fructifications, each of one to six elongate sporangia ringed by sterile axes. Lang & Cookson (1935) read the fructification as a synangium, which would have been remarkable in the Early Devonian. McSweeney et al. (2021) show on one specimen that this was a preservation artefact, so the better-preserved Hedeia (Cookson 1935) is a junior synonym and the plant is a simply branched, rhyniophytoid-grade axis with an unusually elaborate fertile unit. It shows how preservation can split one organism into two form genera, and its possible Silurian status at Yea hangs on a disputed date.

## Morphology and anatomy

I read McSweeney et al. (2021) as reproduced in McSweeney (2024); the 1935 originals were not available. Y. oblonga, the type species, is known from fragments: parent axes 1.0-4.2 mm wide, at least 280 mm tall in the best specimen (NMV P236587), dichotomies rare, daughter axes equal to or slightly narrower than the parent. Fructifications are 3.0-8.4 mm wide and 6.0-17 mm high, fusiform when mature and truncated when immature. Sporangia are 0.7-2.6 mm wide and 2.7-7.0 mm long, borne after one or two closely spaced dichotomies, with tips at about one level in a radial arrangement. Sterile axes outside them divide up to three times and extend up to about 4 mm beyond the sporangial apices, curving over them. Y. corymbosa and Y. sinica (the former Hedeia species) have larger and smaller fructifications respectively; see [Hedeia](hedeia.md). Y. subsphaerica is one specimen with a fructification about as wide as long. Preservation is compression or impression, with rare semi-relief and one cast; SEM of one fructification and axis found no spores, cuticle or tracheids.

## Age and distribution

The type material is from 19 Mile Quarry on the Yarra Track, in the Wilson Creek Shale. Lang & Cookson (1935) dated the flora to the Silurian from graptolites; the shale is now placed in the Pragian-Emsian, with conodonts in the Tyers-Walhalla area from the early Pragian to the early Emsian (Carey & Bolger 1995). Other Victorian specimens come from the Norton Gully Sandstone at Alexandra and Matlock, the Humevale Siltstone at Lilydale and the Yea beds. The Yea records belong to the Lower Plant Assemblage, which Garratt & Rickards (1984) argued was Ludlow on graptolites; Earp (2024) finds the localities west of the Yea anticline Pragian-Emsian, leaves the eastern ones (Limestone Road, Ghin Ghin Road) undated and judges that the evidence does not support a Ludlow age there either. See [the Baragwanathia floras](baragwanathia-flora.md).

Beyond Victoria, Y. sinica occurs in the Pragian Posongchong and Xujiachong formations of Yunnan (Hao & Gensel 1998, as summarised by McSweeney et al. 2021). Gess & Prestianni (2021) assign one Baviaanskloof Formation specimen from South Africa to Y. oblonga; McSweeney et al. suggest it may belong to Mtshaelo kougaensis. Gess & Prestianni say reports from France and Russia need further study, and Fanning et al. (1992) reinterpreted a Welsh Borderland 'Yarravia-type' cluster as globular sporangia split into valves. PBDB also lists Arizona Givetian records (Teichert & Schopf 1958; Canright), which I did not examine. Capel et al. (2023) count Yarravia among the taxa that characterise a north-east Gondwana phytochorion in the Pragian-Emsian.

## Affinity and controversies

Lang & Cookson (1935) found it surprising that the fructification was almost as synangial as the Carboniferous Aulacotheca. Hueber (1983) proposed that Yarravia was a poorly preserved Hedeia. McSweeney et al. (2021) confirmed this when one Eglinton Cutting specimen showed both preservation states, gave Yarravia priority (published 23 March 1935, against 15 July 1935 for Hedeia) and transferred H. corymbosa and H. sinica to it.

Placement is unsettled. McSweeney et al. take the simple branching as excluding the trimerophyte grade (Banks 1975, as summarised by them) and pointing to the [rhyniophytoid](rhyniophytoids.md) grade, but the compound fructification is too elaborate for a rhyniophyte; they read the mismatch as heterobathmy with trimerophyte-like ancestry, which is their inference. Y. subsphaerica may belong to a separate genus.

## Ecology and physiology

Nothing is observed. The plants are drifted fragments in marine rocks; the Eglinton Cutting slab shows many fructifications oriented one way, which probably reflects current alignment (my inference).

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Fertile architecture.** 1-6 sporangia per fructification, each 2.7-7.0 mm long (McSweeney et al. 2021). NMV P236587 has almost 20 fructifications, so one plant plausibly carried tens of sporangia (my arithmetic on observed counts; no spore numbers exist): a concrete spore-output multiplier per fertile axis for F1.
- **Size.** At least 28 cm tall with axes up to 4.2 mm wide in the Pragian-Emsian, narrower than co-occurring Baragwanathia (axes tens of millimetres) but taller than Cooksonia.
- **Timing (F3).** If the Yea records are Early Devonian, no Victorian Yarravia need be older than the Lochkovian-Pragian, and the Silurian outlier among compound fertile units disappears.
- **Record artefacts.** Two genera for one plant inflate genus-level diversity by one, and 'synangium' should not be coded from the type material.
- **Biogeography (F4).** The Yunnan-Victoria link implies exchange or convergence (McSweeney et al. 2021); with no in situ spores it cannot be tied to dispersed spore taxa.

## Open questions

Are the South African, French, Russian and Arizona records Yarravia? Does any Victorian specimen preserve spores or anatomy? Is Y. subsphaerica a separate genus?

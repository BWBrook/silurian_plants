## In brief

The fossil evidence for arbuscular-type symbioses is a ladder of very unequal rungs: dispersed Ordovician hyphae and spores with no host (Palaeoglomus grayi, about 460 Ma); structurally preserved arbuscules and endophytes in rootless Pragian plants at Rhynie, Scotland (about 411 Ma); and Emsian glomeromycete spores in Gaspé. Rhynie also holds Mucoromycotina-like endophytes, so the first partners were not exclusively Glomeromycotina. Experiments on living liverworts quantify what such fungi supply (phosphorus, nitrogen) for carbon, and how the trade changes with CO2.

## Morphology and anatomy

Observed:
- P. grayi: hyphae and spores macerated from Ordovician dolomite that resemble modern glomalean fungi (Redecker et al. 2000, 2002). No arbuscules, no host.
- Aglaophyton majus (Rhynie): aseptate hyphae and arbuscules, morphologically identical to living ones (a basal trunk and a bush-like tuft), in a meristematic cortical zone that kept supplying new host cells (Remy et al. 1994). Glomites rhyniensis has hyphae 8-14 µm wide and smooth multilayered spores 50-80 µm with a 6 µm wall (as compared by Strullu-Derrien et al. 2026). In the Windyfield Chert, Rugososporomyces lavoisierae in Aglaophyton has hyphae 3.5-6.5 µm, spores up to 74 µm (rugose wall about 3.1 µm), vesicles up to 39 µm and arbuscules on stalks about 3.5 µm wide.
- Horneophyton lignieri: Palaeoglomus boullardii in the outer cortex of aerial axes (arbuscule-like structures, vesicles, spores), and Palaeoendogone gwynne-vaughaniae (Mucoromycotina) in the base, in intercellular spaces and as intracellular coils but absent from rhizoids (Strullu-Derrien et al. 2014).
- Nothia aphylla carries three endophytes with host responses (Krings et al. 2007); Archaeosporites rhyniensis propagules under 85 µm are assigned to Archaeosporaceae (Harper et al. 2020).
- Counter-example: the root-bearing Asteroxylon has a probably parasitic cortex fungus, not a mycorrhiza (Strullu-Derrien et al. 2015).

Nutrient exchange itself is inferred, never observed in the fossils.

## Age and distribution

See range notes. Rhynie is dated by U-Pb at 411.5 ± 1.3 Ma (Parry et al. 2011). Retallack (2019, 2022) reports Darriwilian Palaeoglomus strotheri and extensive glomeromycotan networks in Ordovician-Devonian palaeosols; this is single-author and uncorroborated. Molecular clocks put crown embryophytes at 515-474 Ma and Mucoromycota radiations beyond 600 Ma (Berbee et al. 2020, citing Morris et al. 2018), so direct evidence trails plausible origin by 60 Myr or more.

## Affinity and controversies

Ordovician spores. Berbee et al. (2020) note that P. grayi is often cited as the earliest Glomeromycotina but is unassociated with plant fossils and could be modern contamination of porous dolomite. Graham et al. (2017), including an author of the original report, show that glomalean hyphae and spores survive acid hydrolysis, which supports preservability but not age. I know of no independent re-sampling.

Which fungus first. Bidartondo et al. (2011) proposed that Endogone-like Mucoromycotina, not Glomeromycota, enabled land colonisation; Strullu-Derrien et al. (2014) overturned the paradigm that early endophytes were exclusively Glomeromycota. Rimington et al. (2018) found every arbuscular order, with non-Glomeraceae at least 10 times commoner than in flowering plants, in 674 liverworts. Berbee et al. (2020) treat the proposals as non-exclusive and note that Mucoromycotina mycorrhizas evolved several times.

## Ecology and physiology

Rhynie hosts were rootless, at most about 20 cm tall, with fungi in prostrate and aerial axes (Berbee et al. 2020), so the fungus was a likely absorptive surface in nutrient-poor substrate (inference). Genomics supports an ancient toolkit: symbiosis genes arose stepwise before land colonisation (Delaux et al. 2015), and a liverwort shows lipid-for-nutrient exchange with arbuscular fungi (Rich et al. 2021). Mycorrhizas occur in about 85% of living plants (Strullu-Derrien et al. 2018), and mycorrhizal fungi take about 13% of plant primary production (Berbee et al. 2020).

Experiments (living analogues, a few months, nutrient-poor dune soil):
- Field et al. (2012): thalloid liverworts (Preissia quadrata, Marchantia paleacea) with Glomeromycota versus a fern and an angiosperm at 1500 versus 440 ppm CO2. Liverwort phosphorus-per-carbon efficiency fell 10-100-fold at 440 ppm (significant in Preissia) and vascular efficiency rose 10-100-fold. Liverwort active hyphal length was 48% (1500 ppm) and 63% (440 ppm) below vascular plants; 33P uptake fell about 10-fold in Marchantia and 3-4 orders of magnitude in Preissia.
- Field et al. (2015): Haplomitriopsida liverworts with Mucoromycotina fungi form a mutualism whose carbon-for-nutrient efficiency is maintained across both CO2 levels.
- Field et al. (2016, 2019): dual partnerships cost more carbon but gain more nutrient; only Mucoromycotina gave access to 15N from algal necromass, both raised 33P uptake, Glomeromycotina often more effectively. Hoysted et al. (2019): a lycophyte trades carbon for nitrogen with Mucoromycotina.

## Why it matters for modelling

- Treat symbiosis as a host-specific, probabilistic trait with two nutrient channels: Glomeromycotina (phosphorus-weighted) and Mucoromycotina (nitrogen from organic matter), singly or together. Not every Rhynie plant had one (Asteroxylon).
- Cost-benefit depends on CO2 and anatomy. Field et al. (2012) place a roughly 90% CO2 fall in 480-360 Ma; their data imply that fall penalised thalloid, rootless hosts by 1-2 orders of magnitude and rewarded vascular, rooted ones, with liverwort hyphal networks about half the vascular size. That is a candidate selective pressure for conducting tissue and roots; the Mucoromycotina trade appears buffered.
- Calibrate timing with Rhynie (about 411 Ma) as the firm minimum for vascular plant hosts; use the Ordovician spores only as a low-confidence early bound.
- Soil: lichen- and liverwort-dominated crusts bound by cyanobacteria and fungi are the better analogues for early soils, which were thin and limited by host size (Mitchell et al. 2016).

## Open questions

- Is P. grayi really Ordovician, and what produced it?
- Glomeromycotina or Mucoromycotina first in the earliest land plants?
- How much of the host's carbon, and what fraction of axes, did Rhynie fungi take?
- Do Retallack's palaeosol networks stand up to independent test?

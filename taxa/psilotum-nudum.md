## In brief

*Psilotum nudum*, the whisk fern, is a rootless plant with dichotomous green aerial stems carrying minute scale-like appendages, three-lobed synangia, and a subterranean, non-green, fungus-fed gametophyte. Morphological work that continued into the 1980s read it as a possible living relative of rhyniophytes (Brownsey & Lovis 1987). Molecular phylogenies place Psilotaceae among ferns, sister to Ophioglossaceae, so its simplicity is now read as reduction from a more typical fern (Pryer et al. 2001; Nitta et al. 2022; Samec et al. 2019). It still earns a card because some of its physiology has been measured: very low photosynthesis and hydraulic conductance of the whole stem (Franks 2006), a blue-light requirement for stomatal opening (Doi et al. 2015), and a wholly heterotrophic vascularised gametophyte. It is a source of axis-physiology parameters, not a model of ancestral anatomy.

## Morphology and anatomy

Sporophytes are perennial. Aerial stems reach 40-725 mm in New Zealand material (flora factsheet) and up to 36 cm in an Indian epiphytic population (Mandal 2024); unbranched parts are 0.5-3 mm across. The rhizome is achlorophyllous and carries rhizoids; the leaves are scale-like without a midrib or ligule. Steles are a haplostele in the rhizome and an actinostele in the aerial stem (Mandal 2024). Duckett & Ligrone (2005) describe vessel-like elements with scalariform perforation plates and sieve cells in both generations. Synangia are 1-1.5 mm long and 1.5-3 mm wide (factsheet) and contain three fused sporangia (Mandal 2024). Spores are monolete and ellipsoidal, about 30 x 63 micrometres in one unacetolysed garden sample (Pramesti et al. 2023; a second source, relayed there, gives 35 x 76 micrometres). Several of these figures come from floras or single-population studies, not replicated measurement.

## Age and distribution

Pantropical to warm-temperate, growing as an epiphyte, on rock or on the ground. No fossil *Psilotum* was found. The only Psilotaceae macrofossil that Carpenter (1988) knew was *Tmesipteris tasmanica* from the Late Eocene-Oligocene of Tasmania (see [Tmesipteris](tmesipteris.md)); I did not search for later records. Nitta et al. (2022) date the fern crown at 423.2 Ma with an expanded fossil-calibration set, so the whisk-fern stem lineage is probably Palaeozoic, but its fossil record is empty for the window of this programme.

## Affinity and controversies

Brownsey & Lovis (1987) judged a direct link to rhyniophytes more likely on recent morphological evidence, while finding no substantial cytological support for a place within the Filicales. Sequence data reversed this: Pryer et al. (2001) recovered psilotophytes within a horsetail-plus-fern clade, and a fern tree built from plastid data puts Psilotales with Ophioglossales (Ophioglossidae), with horsetails sister to them (Nitta et al. 2022). That last placement is contested, because nuclear data disagree about horsetails. Niklas & Crepet (2022) scored 53 characters for 41 fossil taxa plus *Equisetum*, *Psilotum* and *Tmesipteris* and could not displace Psilotaceae from a poorly resolved zosterophyll-lycophyte clade; known only as fossils, Psilotaceae would probably be classified as a lycophyte lineage. I did not read Bower's or Gensel's older treatments.

## Ecology and physiology

Both generations host intracellular aseptate glomeromycotean fungi with near-identical cytology, and the sporophyte is autotrophic while the gametophyte is not (Duckett & Ligrone 2005). In greenhouse pots, 11 *Glomus* A phylotypes in four clades occurred in gametophytes, sporophytes and neighbouring angiosperm roots; the authors infer carbon flow from photosynthetic plants through shared networks, an inference from fungal identity, not a tracer measurement (Winther & Friedman 2009). Stomatal opening and CO2 fixation both required blue light (Doi et al. 2015). Franks (2006) measured glasshouse-grown, hydrated plants at saturating light, treating whole stems as leaves on projected area: assimilation 2.47 +/- 0.29 micromol m-2 s-1 (wheat 23.5), whole-stem hydraulic conductance 1.30 +/- 0.21 mmol m-2 s-1 MPa-1 (wheat 15.4), and water-potential drawdown 0.22 MPa, the lowest of 10 species.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Axis photosynthesis and hydraulics.** The values above (Franks 2006) are the nearest measurements for a rootless, near-leafless vascular axis, roughly 8-10% of wheat for both assimilation and conductance (derived). They can parameterise or test Rhynie-type steady-state gas-exchange models (see [Aglaophyton](aglaophyton-majus.md)). Limits: present-day CO2, glasshouse plants, a derived fern anatomy.
- **Stomatal control.** Passive control in ferns and lycophytes (Brodribb & McAdam 2011) and a blue-light gate in *Psilotum* (Doi et al. 2015) are not easily reconciled; treat them as alternative modules.
- **Spore size.** The long axis, about 60 micrometres, sits in the range of Rhynie spores (44-65 micrometres, see [Asteroxylon](asteroxylon-mackiei.md)), but the aperture is monolete.
- **Gametophyte nutrition as a trait.** A vascularised gametophyte can be wholly mycoheterotrophic, so tracheid-like cells in Rhynie gametophytes ([Rhynie gametophytes](rhynie-chert-gametophytes.md)) do not by themselves imply autotrophy, and a fungus-fed gametophyte probably needs a photosynthetic donor nearby (inference).
- **Placement by morphology.** Niklas & Crepet (2022) suggest that a reduced fern can look like a lycophyte on morphology alone; simple Devonian axes should not be assumed basal.
- **Ghost lineage.** A probably Palaeozoic stem lineage with one Palaeogene macrofossil is a test case for an observation model.
- **Genome.** Taxa with 1C of about 70 Gbp are known in *Psilotum* (Fernandez et al. 2023); consequences for cell size or growth were not examined in the sources read.

## Open questions

Stomatal density and size (measured by Prats et al. 2024; values not accessed); embolism resistance and axis mechanics; growth rate, and years from spore to sporophyte; spores per synangium, viability and dispersal; carbon transfer to gametophytes by isotope tracer; photosynthesis under elevated CO2; whether thermally heated ground in New Zealand is a useful analogue for the Rhynie hot-spring setting.

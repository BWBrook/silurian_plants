## In brief

Zosterophyllum is the type genus of the zosterophylls: leafless plants with naked axes, H- or K-branched basal rhizomes, and stalked, bivalved sporangia borne laterally in terminal spikes. Its earliest record is late Ludlow (Ludfordian, 425.0-422.7 Ma), it diversifies in the Lochkovian and peaks in the Pragian. The genus is probably not monophyletic, and lycopsids arose from within the zosterophyll grade, so its species show the ancestral body plan from which microphyllous lycopsids evolved. Plant and spike size differ about tenfold among coeval species, which makes it a useful system for life-history trade-offs.

## Morphology and anatomy

Species are known mainly as compressions, with axes 0.3-5.1 mm wide ending in spikes. Spikes in subgenus Zosterophyllum have helically arranged sporangia, whereas Platyzosterophyllum has sporangia oriented to one side of the axis (Gensel 1982; Huang et al. 2025). Sporangia are oval to reniform, and in Z. baoyangense measure 1.6-2.0 mm by 0.9-1.4 mm, with 5-10 per spike; Z. yunnanicum and Z. australianum have a thickened sporangial border (Huang et al. 2025; the 2015 redescription of Z. yunnanicum was not accessed). Z. myretonianum has a single median strand of tracheids (Lang 1927) and was, in 1961, the earliest plant in which cuticle, stomata and complete tracheids had been clearly demonstrated (Lele & Walton 1961). Its basal region was branched mainly by H-type and related patterns, with simple dichotomy rare and the upright fertile axes seldom branched (Lele & Walton 1961). Since the axes carry stomata and there are no leaves or spines, the axes were the main photosynthetic organs (Huang et al. 2025).

The best whole-plant evidence is Z. shengfengense (Hao et al. 2010): an uprooted tuft of aerial axes 98 mm tall on a rhizome that bore upward axes and downward root-like axes about 20 mm long. This is observed for one species; extending it to the genus is an inference.

## Age and distribution

Kotyk et al. (2002) report ten fertile plants of seven taxa, mostly zosterophylls including one Zosterophyllum, from late Ludlow beds of Bathurst Island, dated by conodonts, graptolites and brachiopods; they judged the size and complexity much greater than most contemporaneous fossils. Huang et al. (2025) treat the Yunnan species Z. qujingense as Silurian as well, but Claisse et al. (2025) describe a Pridoli gap in zosterophyll fossils; I could not reconcile the two. Diversity then rose in the Lochkovian (Z. myretonianum in Scotland; Z. shengfengense, about 413 Ma, and Z. xishanense in Yunnan), peaked in the Pragian (Z. australianum from Yunnan, Guizhou and Victoria, Z. rhenanum, Z. confertum, Z. baoyangense) and fell in the Emsian (Cascales-Miñana & Meyer-Berthaud 2014; Huang et al. 2025), a trajectory a model can be tested against. Other records are Z. cf. fertile in the Senni Beds of South Wales (Siegenian-Emsian; Edwards 1969), fertile Z. myretonianum in Scotland (Edwards 1975), Z. australianum in Victoria (Norton Gully Sandstone; McSweeney et al. 2022) and Emsian Z. divaricatum in New Brunswick (Gensel 1982). At least 20 species have been described, at least 14 from South China. The upper Xujiachong Formation, which yields many Chinese species, is dated by spores as early (but not earliest) Pragian to possibly earliest Emsian (Wellman et al. 2012).

## Affinity and controversies

Gensel (1992) argued that lycopsid microphylls and leaf-associated sporangia could be derived from zosterophyll emergences and sporangial position. Recent analyses agree that zosterophylls are a grade paraphyletic to the lycopsids (Nibbelink & Tomescu 2022; Claisse et al. 2025), whereas a 2013 analysis of the Posongchong flora recovered a zosterophyll clade (as summarised by Claisse et al. 2025). Zosterophyllum itself is not a clade: in Claisse et al. (2025) Z. myretonianum falls in a basal group with Discalis and Gumuia, far from the Sawdoniaceae that are sister to the lycopsids. Most species lack anatomy and spores, so species-level relationships are weakly tested (Huang et al. 2025).

## Ecology and physiology

Z. shengfengense was probably semi-aquatic, with either efficient water use or strong uptake by its root-like axes (Hao et al. 2010). H- and K-branched rhizomes imply clonal spread (inference).

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Size and its trend.** Preserved axis lengths average 74 mm in the Lochkovian (range 20-200 mm, n = 10), 120 mm in the Pragian (9.6-440 mm, n = 15) and 87 mm in the Emsian (14-250 mm, n = 6); maximum widths peak at 5.1 mm in the Pragian (Huang et al. 2025).
- **Reproductive allocation.** Huang et al. (2025) estimate a per-spike sporangial volume (TSA, assuming 0.6 mm sporangial thickness): 14.0-101.5 mm³ in Silurian species, 8.7-277.2 mm³ in the Lochkovian, 4.3-499.0 mm³ in the Pragian and 7.4-252.7 mm³ in the Emsian, rising with axial width. In one Pragian bed, small Z. baoyangense (4.3-16.8 mm³) coexisted with Z. australianum (133.8-211.1 mm³), a 12-31-fold difference that they read as r-like versus K-like strategies. The pair gives a measured contrast in body size, inferred lifespan and spore output.
- **Belowground allocation.** Root:shoot ratio is 0.028, with root-like axes about 3% of biomass (Hao et al. 2010), a tiny investment compared with later plants.
- **Photosynthetic area.** Axes were the main photosynthetic organs; each extra millimetre of diameter adds 31.4 mm² per 10 mm of axis (Huang et al. 2025).
- **Calibration.** Morris et al. (2018) used the Ludlow Bathurst Island record as a crown-tracheophyte minimum of 420.7 Ma; Hooper et al. (2026) date crown tracheophytes at 432-428 Ma, so fossils give only minimum ages.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** Contrast size and reproductive allocation among coeval leafless species, using divergent spike volumes while retaining species-specific conducting and rhizomatous architectures.

- **Can transfer:**
  - Belowground allocation in the single uprooted Z. shengfengense whole plant from the Xitun Formation, Yunnan: root:shoot 0.028 and root-like axes about 3% of biomass (Hao et al. 2010).
  - Conducting architecture in Z. myretonianum from the Scottish Carmyllie Beds: a single median tracheid strand; encode anatomy without assigning hydraulic rates (Lang 1927).
  - Near-complete stature in Z. baoyangense from Duyun, Guizhou: 45.4 mm; retain as this species' preserved size rather than a genus-wide height (Huang et al. 2025).
  - Estimated per-spike sporangial volume in Pragian Z. baoyangense from Duyun, Guizhou: 4.3-16.8 mm³ under assumed 0.6 mm sporangial thickness; use as an allocation proxy (Huang et al. 2025).
  - Estimated per-spike sporangial volume in coeval Z. australianum from the Pragian Duyun bed: 133.8-211.1 mm³ under assumed 0.6 mm sporangial thickness; keep separate from Z. baoyangense (Huang et al. 2025).
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - Genus-wide rooting architecture, biomass allocation or clonal-spread rate cannot be fixed from Z. shengfengense; the complete rooting system represents one species and one specimen.
  - Measured lifespan and spore output cannot be assigned to Z. baoyangense or Z. australianum from their estimated sporangial volumes; both quantities remain inferred.
  - A maximum complete height of 440 mm cannot be assigned to Z. confertum; the measurement describes preserved axes, with larger true heights possible.
  - A secure Pridoli occurrence cannot be assigned from Z. qujingense without resolving the conflict between its reported Silurian age and the proposed Pridoli gap.
  - Genus-wide spore morphology and securely observed homospory cannot be assigned from the front matter; in situ spore evidence is sparse (Huang et al. 2025).

## Open questions

Which species belong with the type species? Are the Pridoli records (Z. qujingense) real, or is the gap genuine? Spore type is unknown for most species. Were the small r-like species colonisers of disturbed alluvium? Does the single complete rooting system (Z. shengfengense) generalise, and what water uptake does a 3% root fraction imply?

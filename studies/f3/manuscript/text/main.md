# Missing fossils as evidence: what the rocks can say about the age of land plants

<div id="authors"></div>

## Abstract

Molecular clocks date living land plants' ancestor from the Cambrian to Late Ordovician,
partly through differing fossil maxima. We examined three: 515.5 million years ago (Ma), resting on
qualitative argument, and 459.1 Ma for living land plants; 431.2 Ma for living vascular plants. No
three-rayed (trilete) spores are reported in Ordovician Libyan borehole samples[]{.C01}, most from stages
with such spores elsewhere[]{.C45}[]{.C54}: absence can be local. For vascular plants, the rule of three
applies: if fossils turn up at a steady rate after the oldest, the silence before it fits any
history expected to yield about three finds or fewer at that rate[]{.C58}. The fewer finds that are
independent discoveries, the longer three take; at a steady rate the 95% limit on the record's
start is [428.8]{.C27 .V003} Ma if all [48]{.C25} are, near 431.2 Ma if half are. Ancestors at
444–452 Ma fit if early fossils were found [2.4]{.C48 .V056}–[12]{.C48 .V055} times less often,
depending on that count, if finding rose fast enough, or if recognisable bodies appeared later.
In the late Silurian, fossils already turned up at near their later average rate[]{.C60}, whereas North
American land-laid rock and the share of studies reporting trilete spores rose later, so neither matches
the pattern of finds[]{.C59}.

**Keywords:** molecular clock; fossil calibration; land plants; vascular plants; stratigraphic
confidence interval; Ordovician–Silurian

## Introduction

Molecular clocks estimate when lineages diverged by combining DNA differences with fossil limits on the
ages of selected ancestors. A crown group comprises the most recent ancestor shared by its living
members and all its descendants, including extinct lineages. Clocks date these crown ancestors;
extinct lineages outside the crown may have reached land earlier. For the ancestor of all living land plants
(crown embryophytes), Morris et al. [@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand] obtained 515–474 Ma across seven
tree topologies and Harris et al. [@harris2022DivergentEvolutionaryTrajectories] 515–493 Ma. Hooper et
al. [@hooper2026ReconstructingSporeEvolution] reanalysed Harris et al.'s DNA data with revised fossil
calibrations and obtained 457–450 Ma, a mean 55 Myr younger. For the ancestor of living vascular plants
(crown tracheophytes: clubmosses and their relatives, ferns and seed plants), the three studies give
451–419, 452–447 and 432–428 Ma (figure 1); Su et al. [@su2021LargescalePhylogenomicAnalyses] obtained
older land-plant ages still, under a Proterozoic maximum. The dates bear on the order in which plant
characters were assembled, and on how early plants changed productivity, weathering and climate
[@kenrick2012TimelineTerrestrializationConsequences; @donoghue2021EvolutionaryEmergenceLand;
@strother2021FossilRecordLand; @lenton2012FirstPlantsCooled].

A fossil sets a minimum age: its lineage existed by the age of its oldest secure member. A maximum
based on absence adds an argument that older rocks would probably have preserved and yielded
recognisable remains had the lineage existed [@benton2006PaleontologicalEvidenceDate]. How calibrations
are specified can strongly shape estimated dates, and with large sequence datasets they can dominate
the uncertainty in absolute ages [@ho2009AccountingCalibrationUncertainty;
@dosreis2015BayesianMolecularClock]. A soft maximum permits older ages through a specified tail of the
calibration prior [@yang2005BayesianEstimationSpecies]. Whether absence can set a maximum is contested
[@hedges2018AccurateTimetreesRequire; @morris2018ReplyHedgesEt], and best practice asks each calibration
to justify its maximum from the relevant geological, geographical and taphonomic record
[@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying].

Linking a fossil to an ancestor crosses two intervals: from divergence to the first recognisable
character, and from that character to its first recorded fossil [@marshall2019UsingFossilRecord]. Both
are uncertain for early land plants. Dispersed spores appear tens of millions of years before branched
plant bodies [@wellman2000MicrofossilRecordEarly; @rubinstein2010EarlyMiddleOrdovician;
@steemans2009OriginRadiationEarliest], terrestrial deposits are scarce before the latest Silurian, at
least in western Europe [@kenrick2012TimelineTerrestrializationConsequences], and the oldest parent
plants known in detail are minute [@edwards2014CryptosporesCryptophytesReveal].

Three maxima divide the current clocks (table 1). Morris et al. set 515.5 Ma for living land plants,
arguing that older marine rocks rich in algal cysts would also have yielded land-plant spores
[@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand; @morris2018ReplyHedgesEt]. Hooper et al. set 459.1 Ma from the oldest
possible trilete (three-rayed) spores, in the Borenshult-1 core of southern Sweden
[@rubinstein2019BalticaCradleEarly], and 431.2 Ma for living vascular plants from Irish *Cooksonia*
[@edwards1980RecordsCooksoniatypeSporangia]. The three therefore rest on a preservation analogy, on
dispersed spores and on plant bodies, each with its own route into the record, and each needs its own
test.

We ask three questions of the published record. What evidence supports each maximum, and what links
connect it to the dated ancestor? What can the absence of spores from one Libyan basin show? How much less often would early vascular-plant fossils have to be found, or how long
after the ancestor would recognisable bodies have to appear, for older dates to fit? Established theory
sets an expectation for each. An argument from absence needs documented chances for remains to be
delivered, preserved and found [@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying; @marshall2019UsingFossilRecord].
Where older and younger rocks were sampled comparably, each further sample reporting no target spores
tightens the limit on a high, steady spore supply. With the oldest find and the time window fixed, more independent finds bring
the limit on a record's start closer to that find, and a lower finding rate before it pushes the limit
back [@strauss1989ClassicalConfidenceIntervals; @marshall1990ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic]. The
audit identifies what each maximum assumes, the Libyan record shows how far a spore absence reaches, and
the body-fossil models describe the finding rates or delays that would fit older vascular-plant dates.

## Material and methods

**(a) Timescale and interpretations.** Analysed ages use the stage bases of the International
Chronostratigraphic Chart, v2024/12 [@cohen2013IcsInternationalChronostratigraphic]; literature ages
were mapped by stage. Published maxima keep their source timescale, GTS2012, so they can be compared as
their authors set them. Each analysis declares which interpretation of disputed identifications it
uses, main or inclusive, so contested fossils enter as alternatives.

**(b) Source audit.** For each maximum we traced the calibration fossil, its age rule and how it was
found back to the original descriptions, and recorded each published interpretation of the fossil's
identity with its evidence [@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying]. A reported absence applies to the
material examined, and a missing report counts as an absence where the source reports the target fully.

**(c) Libyan spore records.** Abuhmida [@abuhmida2013PalynologicalAnalysisOrdovician] reported spores
and other microfossils from Ordovician to lower Silurian wells in the Murzuq Basin, southwest Libya. We
transcribed all [164]{.C38} appendix samples with their chart and prose reports. The analysis uses the
chart-coded samples of drill cuttings (rock chips returned during drilling) from five wells: three supply
[28]{.C01} samples older than the Llandovery (earliest Silurian), and all five supply [59]{.C02}
Llandovery samples, which serve as the reference. The source states the absence of trilete spores
explicitly for part of the sequence; for the Hawaz Formation, samples reporting none rest on complete reporting
charts and the stated microscopy protocol. The basin lay near the Ordovician South Pole, within the area
of Hirnantian glaciation [@cocks2021OrdovicianPalaeogeographyClimate;
@scotese2021PhanerozoicPaleotemperaturesEarths] (electronic supplementary material, figure S2). We
compared the samples' ages with trilete records in our spore compilation, built from Palynodata
[@white2008PalynodataDatafile2006], the curated assemblages of Wellman et al.
[@wellman2013Chapter29Palaeophytogeography] and later literature, keeping records with usable ages that
are not flagged as reworked or questionable. A model of spore supply, in which samples from one well
share a well effect, sets upper limits on how much steady supply the samples allow; its 95% limits were
tested on simulated data with known answers (electronic supplementary material, S2).

**(d) Body-fossil finds.** We assembled land-plant body fossils dated to 453–393 Ma from Paleobiology
Database (PBDB) occurrences, whose names an AI model classified with cited sources before our audit, and
from a source-reviewed set of the oldest records with their competing interpretations. Collections, taxa
or papers sharing a locality and age count as one find, a proxy for an independent fossil horizon. We
declare three nested categories. Land-plant bodies are any accepted macro- or mesofossil.
Polysporangiophytes have documented branched axes bearing sporangia (spore capsules), or placement
within the vascular crown. Crown vascular plants belong to the lycophyte line (clubmosses and relatives)
or the euphyllophytes (ferns and seed plants) [@kotyk2002MorphologicallyComplexPlant;
@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand; @earp2024NotanopliidBrachiopodsAssociated;
@libertin2024GenusAberlemniaSiluriandevonian]. Under the main interpretation the three categories hold
[78]{.C25}, [71]{.C25} and [50]{.C25} finds, of which [76]{.C40 .V021}, [69]{.C25} and [48]{.C25} are
certainly older than the end of the Emsian ([393.47]{.C25} Ma), the younger edge of the time window
(electronic supplementary material, S5.1).

**(e) Limit on when the record began.** Let *w*(*s*) be a fixed relative rate at which recognisable
fossils of a category are found at age *s*, and $U(a)=\int_c^a w(s)\,ds$ the cumulative finding rate
between a younger cutoff *c*, here the end of the Emsian, and age *a*. For *n* independent finds with
oldest age *A*, the 95% confidence limit *T* on the start of the recognisable record
solves $U(T)=U(A)\,0.05^{-1/n}$ (figure 2). With constant *w* this is the classical confidence interval
on a stratigraphic range, and the absolute rate cancels [@strauss1989ClassicalConfidenceIntervals;
@marshall1990ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic]; varying *w* follows the extensions to unevenly spaced
fossil horizons [@marshall1994ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic;
@marshall1997ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic]. For finds dated by age ranges, *A* is the oldest
possible age of the oldest find, and *n* counts only finds certainly inside the window; both choices
move the limit older. Finds dated by stage may record one discovery more than once, so we report the limit as a
function of the number of independent finds, with the main count, a count of region–stage cells and the
curated oldest records as reference points. The limit concerns the start of the record; the Discussion
treats its link to ancestors.

**(f) Finding-rate histories and shape tests.** We compared six assumed histories of the finding rate:
steady; proportional to PBDB land and marginal-marine collections per Myr
[@brook2026FrozenPaleobiologyDatabase], with or without the collections that hold plant fossils;
declining exponentially into the past with 25 or 10 Myr timescales; and plant-excluded collection
density multiplied by the 10 Myr decline. The collection records reach back to [469.4]{.C27 .V007} Ma,
the oldest age the analysis can consider. For each history, an exact Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compared
the spread of fossil ages it predicts with the observed spread, given the count and the oldest find.
Because many ages are known only as ranges, we searched every possible assignment of ages within those
ranges, including whether the finds straddling the cutoff fall inside the window, for the best fit
[compare @berger1994PValuesMaximized]. A history that fails under every assignment is rejected
(electronic supplementary material, S5.2).

**(g) Lower earlier finding rates and delays.** The main scenario holds the finding rate steady after
[426.7]{.C32} Ma, the oldest possible age of a crown vascular-plant find, and lowers it before that
age. For a candidate ancestor age *t* and an assumed delay *d* before the first recognisable bodies, the
ratio of earlier to later finding rate that puts *t* − *d* where the limit of (*e*) would fall is
$r=(A-c)(0.05^{-1/n}-1)/(t-d-A)$, defined where $t-d>A$; fossils would then have to be found $1/r$ times
less often before the oldest find than after it. The longer the delay, the smaller the reduction a given
ancestor age needs [@marshall2019UsingFossilRecord]. If fossils turn up at a steady rate after *A* and recognisable bodies appear with the ancestor (zero
delay), a candidate age *t* fits the oldest find (an oldest find no
older than *A* has probability at least 0.05) when the relative finding rate summed over [*A*, *t*] is at most $K=(A-c)(0.05^{-1/n}-1)$. Writing *R* for
that sum, each of the *n* finds falls before *A* with probability $p=R/(A-c+R)$; at the boundary $R=K$,
no early find has probability $(1-p)^n=0.05$, so $p\approx3/n$: the rule of three for zero counts
[@hanley1983IfNothingGoes]. At the later rate estimated as $n/(A-c)$ finds per Myr,
*K* corresponds to $np/(1-p)=n(0.05^{-1/n}-1)$ expected finds before the oldest find, which approaches
$\ln 20\approx3$ as *n* grows. The step is one shape among many; we also report a linear rise from zero at the ancestor and an exponential rise, each reaching
the later level at *A*. We chose where to put the step and the rises after seeing the data, so these
curves show what would fit, without the 95% confidence that (*e*) gives each fixed history.

**(h) Outside measures.** To judge whether a required reduction is large, we compared it with three
rough measures, defined before they were computed: the area of North American Macrostrat columns
holding land-laid (non-marine) rock [@peters2018MacrostratPlatformGeological]; PBDB land and
marginal-marine collections per Myr [@brook2026FrozenPaleobiologyDatabase]; and the share of Palynodata
studies [@white2008PalynodataDatafile2006] that report trilete spores. Each measure's mean over the
33 Myr after the oldest find, divided by its mean from the oldest find back to a candidate ancestor age,
gives how many times scarcer it was earlier, at zero delay. The measures are guides; they enter no 95%
inference (electronic supplementary material, S5.8). Each covers the same two windows as the
finding-rate requirement, so the two ratios can be compared in size. After seeing the single measures,
we also multiplied the rock and spore measures stage by stage, as a further
guide without confidence level, and asked whether the later finds lie where each measure is large: a
measure that finding followed stage by stage would put most later finds where it is high. We kept
searching out of the product, because collections are made in rock and the product could count rock
availability twice, and multiplied it with the spore measure instead as a global alternative.
For finding proportional to a combined measure, its integrals $I_E$ over [$A,t$] and $I_L$ over [$c,A$]
give a no-early-find probability $[I_L/(I_L+I_E)]^n$ and an expected early count $nI_E/I_L$ at the
fitted later intensity. Multiplying the two separate ratios instead gives a different assumed contrast
between mean finding rates. For later placement, we compare the minimum count whose whole age range
lies within an interval with a binomial distribution whose event probability is that interval's share
of the measure's later-window integral.

## Results

**(a) The 515.5 Ma maximum rests on a qualitative argument.** Morris et al. placed this maximum below the
oldest possible total-group records, spore-like packets and clusters from the Cambrian Stage 4 Rome
Formation, at the base of the *Olenellus* Biozone (about [515.5]{.C46} Ma). Their preservation argument,
from older marine rocks with algal cysts and without land-plant spores, shows that walled microfossils
survived and were recovered [@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand; @morris2018ReplyHedgesEt]. How many spores
reached those basins, the land areas they drained and the search effort remain unmeasured in the sources
we assessed[]{.C16}. All [three]{.C15} sources we examined assign the Cambrian packets to algae or
lineages before land plants: Charophyta [@strother2016SystematicsEvolutionarySignificance], a
pre-embryophytic grade [@taylor2024UltrastructureCambrianCryptospores] or ancestral aeroterrestrial
algae [@wang2022PalynologicalAssemblageCambrian] (electronic supplementary material, S4).

**(b) The younger maxima follow a stage-base rule.** Hooper et al. set both younger maxima as a GTS2012
stage base plus its uncertainty. They keyed 459.1 Ma ([458.4 + 0.7 = 459.1]{.C42} Ma) to a possible
trilete spore at 48.2 m in the Borenshult-1 core [@rubinstein2019BalticaCradleEarly]. The oldest
definite trilete level, at 43.1 m, is also Sandbian and lies below the [454.41]{.C43} Ma Kinnekulle
K-bentonite, so the rule gives the same value from either level (electronic supplementary material,
S2.4). They keyed 431.2 Ma ([430.5 + 0.7 = 431.2]{.C47} Ma) to Irish Homerian *Cooksonia*
[@edwards1980RecordsCooksoniatypeSporangia]. The older Czech *Cooksonia barrandei*, a branched
sporophyte with terminal sporangia from a Sheinwoodian (early Silurian) horizon dated from its slab fauna
and by correlation [@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand], meets the same criterion (electronic
supplementary material, table S13). On the current timescale the rule gives outer values of
[431.9]{.C47} Ma for the Homerian and [434.1]{.C47} Ma for the Sheinwoodian.

**(c) The Libyan samples show that a spore absence can be local.** Trilete spores are reported in
[51 of 59]{.C02} Llandovery samples and in none of the [28]{.C01} older samples (electronic
supplementary material, figure S2). Of the older samples, [18]{.C45}, from three wells, are
Katian–Hirnantian (Late Ordovician). They are younger than the definite Swedish triletes below the
[454.41]{.C43} Ma bentonite, and fall in the same stages as trilete-bearing assemblages in Saudi
Arabia and Turkey [@steemans2009OriginRadiationEarliest; @steemans1996OrdovicianSilurianCryptospores][]{.C54}
(electronic supplementary material, S2.6). [5]{.C45} of the Libyan samples, and a Saudi assemblage, are Hirnantian, a stage of
about two million years. Trilete spores thus occur elsewhere in the stages these Libyan rocks
represent, and none is reported from them. Only [10]{.C45} broadly dated Darriwilian (Middle Ordovician) samples, from two wells, have age
ranges reaching beyond 459.1 Ma, and their weight depends on how ages are spread within the
stage[]{.C09}. With equal delivery, the samples limit steady trilete supply since 459.1 Ma to
[0.091]{.C24} of the Llandovery level under the main model, and to about [0.4]{.C51 .V081} when
samples are weighted by well and rock unit (electronic supplementary material, S2.2 and S2.5). Lower
delivery, lower local supply or producers outside the catchment all fit.

**(d) The silence before the oldest find fits about three expected finds, so its limit depends on how
many finds are independent.** Every calculation in which fossils turn up at a steady rate after the
oldest find shares one rule. Take that rate, estimated from the count: the absence of older finds fits
any earlier history expected to yield about three finds or fewer at that rate[]{.C58}, from [3.09]{.C58 .V178} with 48 independent finds to
[3.32]{.C58 .V179} with 15 (Material and methods (*g*)). The count sets how long three finds take at the later
rate: [2.1]{.C57 .V112} Myr with 48 finds, [5.7]{.C57 .V113} Myr with 19 and [7.3]{.C57 .V114} Myr
with 15. At a steady finding rate, the 95% confidence limit on the start of the crown vascular-plant
record lies that far before the oldest find (figure 3*a*). Counting the
[48]{.C25} finds certainly inside the window as independent gives [428.8]{.C27 .V003} Ma; treating each
region and stage as one find ([19]{.C30}) gives [432.4]{.C30} Ma, and counting only the curated oldest
records ([15]{.C30}) gives [434.05]{.C30} Ma. The three share the oldest find and the cutoff, so they
lie on one curve[]{.C55}, which crosses the 431.2 Ma maximum at about [24]{.C55 .V109} independent finds,
half the main count. The main count already merges repeated collections from one locality and age; the
region-and-stage count treats every locality in a region and stage as one discovery, and the curated
count keeps only the source-reviewed oldest records. These are counting scenarios, not estimates of the number of independent finds; the curve shows which
count each limit assumes. Coarse stage ages are one reason the count is uncertain: finds from one region and stage may come from one
bed or from beds millions of years apart. Over-counting is a known way for the method to fail. In
simulations in which every true find was counted twice, the 95% limit held in only [0.78]{.C34} of
trials (electronic supplementary material, S5.6). If the 48 records instead represent 24 independent
paired finds, their conditional steady-rate limit is near the 431.2 Ma maximum.

With all 48 finds independent, finding rates that track collection density give [428.9]{.C27 .V009}
Ma, or [428.5]{.C27 .V010} Ma with plant-bearing collections removed, and a 25 Myr decline into the past
gives [431.6]{.C27 .V011} Ma. If finding fossils became steeply harder with age (the 10 Myr decline),
the method sets no limit as far back as [469.4]{.C27 .V007} Ma, the edge of our data. That history,
however, predicts a spread of fossil ages unlike the one observed, however the uncertain ages are
assigned[]{.C49}. The 25 Myr decline passes the shape test under one assignment of ages when all
[50]{.C49 .V107} possible finds are counted (*p* = [0.067]{.C49 .V104}), and fails under every
assignment of the [48]{.C49 .V069} finds certainly inside the window (best *p* = [0.035]{.C49 .V106}).
Shifting stage boundaries by their quoted uncertainties, in the scenarios tested, moves the main limit
between [427.31]{.C50 .V062} and [430.42]{.C50 .V063} Ma (electronic supplementary material, S5.3).

**(e) Older vascular-plant dates fit if early fossils were found less often, or recognisable bodies
appeared later.** In the main scenario, halving the finding rate before the oldest find moves the limit
to [431.0]{.C53 .V108} Ma, still younger than the 431.2 Ma maximum. Ancestors at 444–452 Ma, spanning
the Morris et al. mean dates and the Harris et al. interval, fit if fossils were found
[8]{.C48 .V054}–[12]{.C48 .V055} times less often before the oldest find than after it, with
recognisable bodies appearing with the ancestor and all 48 finds independent (figure 3*b*). These factors
spread three finds' worth of finding over the 17–25 Myr between the ancestor and the oldest find. With about
24 independent finds, [3.9]{.C55 .V110}–[5.7]{.C55 .V111} times suffices, and with 19 or 15,
[2.4]{.C48 .V056}–[4.5]{.C48 .V057} times; the reduction needed grows roughly in proportion to the
number of independent finds, and with fewer than about 24, 431.2 Ma already fits a steady rate. At a
steady finding rate and with all 48 finds independent, the same ancestors fit if recognisable bodies
appeared about [15–23]{.C33} Myr or more after them, leaving at most the [2.1]{.C57 .V112} Myr that three
finds take; fewer independent finds shorten the delay needed. Smaller reductions combine with shorter delays (figure 3*b*).

**(f) The record limits early finding to about three finds in total, whatever its shape.** With fossils
turning up at a steady rate after *A* and zero delay, a straight-line rise from zero at the ancestor stays
within the three-find total of (*d*) only for ancestors up to [431.0]{.C57 .V115}, [438.1]{.C57 .V116} and
[441.4]{.C57 .V117} Ma with 48, 19 and 15 finds. An exponential rise fits ancestors at
444–452 Ma if finds doubled about every [1.5]{.C57 .V118} Myr or faster with 48 finds, every [4.0]{.C57 .V119}–[4.2]{.C57 .V120}
Myr with 19 and every [5.3]{.C57 .V121}–[5.8]{.C57 .V122} Myr with 15. As candidate ages grow older, the permitted
doubling time approaches a count-specific threshold, $K\ln 2$, about [1.48]{.C57 .V123}, [3.93]{.C57 .V124} and
[5.09]{.C57 .V125} Myr; a rise this fast or faster fits an ancestor of any age, because its whole early
tail sums to about three finds or fewer. These are requirements for fitting. For coarse context,
the share of palynological studies reporting triletes changed between the Homerian and the Gorstian,
across the oldest find, at an equivalent doubling time of about [6.6]{.C57 .V126} Myr, and rose fastest, about
every [1.0]{.C57 .V127} Myr, from the Hirnantian into the Rhuddanian; both give scale only.

**(g) The measures that fall enough before the oldest find fail where the later record tests them.**
Two outside measures fell enough before the oldest find to bear on older ancestors. For ancestors at
444–452 Ma, the mean area of North American columns holding non-marine units is about
[3.2]{.C56 .V128}–[3.5]{.C56 .V129} times lower before the oldest find than after it, the share of indexed
palynological studies reporting trilete spores [5.1]{.C56 .V132}–[7.1]{.C56 .V133} times lower, and their
stage-by-stage product, a combination we chose after seeing both, [21.7]{.C59 .V185}–[29.8]{.C59 .V186} times
lower, enough to fit those ancestors at every count. PBDB land and marginal-marine collections are only
[1.35]{.C56 .V130}–[1.41]{.C56 .V131} times sparser (figure 3*b*; electronic supplementary material, S5.8 and
S5.10). If finding had followed a measure, the later finds would lie where that measure is high. Finding
that followed rock area, the spore share or their product would put [0.45]{.C59 .V199},
[3.8]{.C59 .V200} and [0.14]{.C59 .V201} finds in the Gorstian to Pridoli, where at least [13]{.C59 .V198} of
the 48 lie; a level rate puts [10.2]{.C59 .V203} there and searching [10.1]{.C59 .V204}. A measure that
fails where the record can test it gives little reason to trust it before the oldest find, where the
record cannot, and the one measure compatible with the later finds, searching, falls far too little to
fit older ancestors[]{.C59}.

**(h) In the late Silurian, finding was already near its later average.** The late-Silurian finds, none
of them among the units that set *A*, put the average finding rate over the Gorstian to Pridoli at no
less than [0.79]{.C60} of the later average with all 48 finds independent, or [0.69]{.C60} counting by
region and stage (one-sided 95% bounds). Among exponential histories, finding rose at most about
[1.8]{.C60}-fold, or [2.4]{.C60}-fold, across the following 33 Myr. The finds come from six regions, from
Laurentia and Baltica to South China and South America. Before the oldest find, (*d*) allows about three
finds' worth in total, so with zero delay the simple rises that fit older ancestors climb steeply to the
later level near *A*: a straight-line rise from zero within [4.3]{.C60} Myr with 48 finds and
[14.7]{.C60} Myr with 15, or an exponential rise doubling about every [1.5]{.C57 .V118}–[5.8]{.C57 .V122} Myr
or faster (*f*). A delay before recognisable bodies gives the same pattern. Rock area and the spore share
change elsewhere: both stay low through the late Silurian and rise in the Lochkovian (electronic
supplementary material, S5.10).

**(i) One Ordovician find moves the broader land-plant limit.** Sporangial fragments with in situ
cryptospores from the Ghaba-1 borehole, Oman, dated to the Katian on later correlation
[@wellman2003FragmentsEarliestLand], form one land-plant body find. Adding them moves the steady-rate
limit for all land-plant body fossils from [434.5]{.C28} to [455.2]{.C40 .V022} Ma, and the enlarged
set fails the shape test under all six histories[]{.C49}. The fragments came from palynological
preparation of borehole material, whereas most later finds are compressions collected from bedding
planes [@edwards1979RecordsFertileRhyniophytina].

## Discussion

The early record allows older ancestors, and the analyses state what each would require. The evidence
differs in depth between the two clades. For living land plants it comes from the audit of the 515.5 Ma
maximum and one polar basin, whose samples report no triletes from stages in which triletes occur
elsewhere. Those samples exclude a steady, well-delivered supply near the Llandovery level, while lower
delivery, lower local supply or spore producers outside the catchment all fit. For living vascular plants it comes from a body
record drawn from several regions: at a steady finding rate, the 95%
confidence limit on the start of the recognisable crown vascular-plant record lies between
[429]{.C27 .V018} and [434.05]{.C30} Ma, depending on how many finds are independent, and passes the
431.2 Ma maximum at about half the main count. For each way of counting, a lower earlier finding rate, later recognisable
bodies or both fit ancestors older than these limits; the models give the size of each requirement in
aggregate. If fossils turned up at a steady rate after the oldest find and recognisable bodies appeared with the
ancestor, the record allows about three finds' worth of earlier
finding, so it limits the total of early finding, and every history with that total fits equally. A
fast enough exponential rise keeps that total below three for an ancestor of any age, so an older limit
from the body record needs an assumption that restricts the early finding history.

Several processes could have made early plants scarcer in the record. Producers may have been rarer,
more geographically restricted, concentrated where little terrestrial sediment accumulated, or preserved
in intervals that have been searched less. Body size, cuticle and lignified tissue could also raise the
chance that a body is preserved, found and recognised. Branched sporophytes and conducting tissue were
acquired stepwise along the lineages being dated [@kenrick1997OriginEarlyEvolution]. The early branched
sporophyte *C. barrandei* is preserved in marine sediments [@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand],
and the Oman fragments show that small sporangia can survive [@wellman2003FragmentsEarliestLand]. The
models give the combined reduction a candidate age requires. The outside measures show less column
area holding land-laid rock in North America and a lower share of indexed
palynological studies reporting trilete spores, which changing coverage, study aims, preservation and
identification can also lower. These differences give the required ratios a scale, and multiplied they would fit ancestors at
444–452 Ma at every count. Finding that followed either measure, or their product, would have left far fewer
late-Silurian finds than the 13 observed. The measures fail this later-record check, so they give little
support to an earlier fall in finding; the pooled late-Silurian finds instead show finding already near its
later average, and those finds come from six regions.

*Interpretation.* The rest of this paragraph is interpretation beyond the tests. The record looks like a
switch: one or more evolutionary innovations may have triggered a rapid spread into all the habitat open
to these plants, with no further innovation needed. Population multiplication alone could be effectively instantaneous on these timescales, although
geographical spread would also depend on dispersal and connected habitat. Even a population growing by 1% a year doubles in about
70 years, whereas the record allows rises in finding that double about every 1.5–5.8 Myr or faster for
ancestors at 444–452 Ma (Results (*f*)), so a rise that slow would need something besides population
growth to pace it: evolution, environment, preservation or searching. Fast growth fills only the habitat that is open, and a lineage can persist small and rare
for a long time, so a switch dates the spread, not the lineage. The reading forks on the trigger. If it
was a feature both living branches inherited, the branches split after it, and their ancestor is little
older than the first crown fossils. If the trigger came after the split, or was environmental, the
ancestor can be much older. This paper leaves the fork open; a planned study of which traits the
earliest fossils combine, and in what order they appear, bears on it. Three cautions apply. *Cooksonia*,
a widespread early form, is a morphotype that groups simple plants from more than one lineage
[@kenrick2012TimelineTerrestrializationConsequences], so the apparent sameness of early floras partly
reflects how few features they show. Many early specimens are fragments that drifted into marine or
near-shore sediments [@edwards1979RecordsFertileRhyniophytina; @libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand],
so the habitats the spread filled are inferred. And *Cooksonia*-type sporangia hold trilete spores
[@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand], while trilete spores appear tens of millions of years
earlier [@hooper2026ReconstructingSporeEvolution], so plants with such spores but no known body lived
long before the switch. A change in preservation, such as the spread of tissues that fossilise well,
would look the same in this record.

Silvestro et al. [@silvestro2015RevisitingOriginDiversification] modelled genus-level PBDB plant
occurrences with a joint Bayesian model of preservation and diversification, and estimated the origin of
vascular plants at a mean of 433.5 Ma (95% highest posterior density 449.0–424.0 Ma). They estimate when
the earliest vascular-plant genus lineage arose, with a fitted, varying preservation rate; we limit the
start of the recognisable record of a declared crown category under assumed finding-rate histories, then
ask what older ancestors require. The two analyses target different quantities from partly shared data
and test different assumptions. CladeDate builds calibration densities directly from fossil times
[@claramunt2022CladedateCalibrationInformation], and Hooper et al. link spore-character reconstruction
to revised calibrations. We make the maxima's observational requirements explicit, limiting local spore
supply and sizing the reductions in finding rate, or the delays, that fit older vascular-plant dates.

**Conditions.** The Libyan limits assume that the Llandovery spores are indigenous, that older and
younger samples were reported comparably, that ages are uniform within stages, and that samples are
independent apart from a shared well effect; the four-tenths figure comes from an exploratory weighting
that was not tested on simulated data (electronic supplementary material, S2.5). The comparison with
other regions holds at stage resolution and depends on the coverage of the spore compilation. Linking the local spore record to
ancestors requires that the earliest crown embryophytes produced recognisable trilete spores, that the
trait persisted and that producers lived in the catchment (electronic supplementary material, S6).

The body-fossil limits and shape rejections assume independent ages on a continuous scale; fixed
finding-rate histories, cutoff, timescale, inventory and counting rules; and an observation process
whose reported age ranges always contain the true ages, including whether each find falls inside the
window. Geological age ranges carry no demonstrated joint coverage; if the probability that any range
misses its true age is $\delta$, the test's error rate is at most $\alpha+\delta$, where $\alpha$ is its
level for exact ages. Simulations with exact ages
gave coverage of [0.941–0.958]{.C34} (electronic supplementary material, S5.6). The step scenario was chosen after
seeing the data. Linking the vascular-plant limit to ancestors requires that the oldest finds belong to
the crown group, and a specified delay before recognisable bodies. The rule of three counts expected
early finds at the later rate estimated from the same finds, so it is conditional on the count and the
cutoff, and a delay moves the start of that count to *t* − *d*. The rises and the
window-mean reading of the outside comparison assume a level finding rate after the oldest find, which
the spread of later fossil ages can test, while finding proportional to a measure lets the later rate
vary with it; the doubling times are requirements, and the spread of early plants is unmeasured. The outside
measures describe rock and published studies; each ratio would equal the ratio of mean finding rates if
finding rate were proportional to that measure, with the same constant in both windows. The placement
check contradicts finding proportional to the rock measure, the spore share or their product within the
later window; a level rate and searching remain compatible with the Gorstian–Pridoli allocation at the
minimum count, a result that depends on uncertain ages and the interval chosen. Reading any measure's contrast as a finding-rate contrast assumes that mean finding rates share it
between the windows, which the placement check gives no reason to expect for rock or spores. Each
placement check compares one late-Silurian allocation, using the 13 finds (six region-and-stage units)
dated wholly within it, while up to 16 may lie there, independent units and age
ranges that contain the true ages, and inherits any imbalance in how the inventory sampled Silurian and
Devonian records. Both placement intervals and the oldest-age boundary were chosen from the observed
age ranges, so the binomial tails describe fixed-window discrepancies rather than calibrated rejection
probabilities after that choice. Treating regional cells as independent draws is another counting
scenario. The stage-permutation comparison assumes exchangeable labels and leaves temporal dependence
out of account. The combined measure was chosen after the single ones were seen, has no confidence
level, and may count a shared cause twice. The switch reading uses the same minimum counts, nine of the
13 dated only to the Pridoli, and one exponential family for the later history; targeted searching of the oldest
plant-bearing beds, which collection density does not measure, could raise the late-Silurian count.
Its bounds are interval averages, and its durations apply to the simple histories examined: the
straight-line rise spans at most a few million years to about 15, depending on the count. It concerns
the finding of recognisable crown bodies, not when plants or the lineage arose.

Published evidence could narrow these requirements. Records of searched Silurian terrestrial beds, with
effort and empty beds included, would help constrain early finding rates, and rock-unit compilations
beyond North America would test the rock measure [@peters2018MacrostratPlatformGeological].
A second complete spore sequence outside polar Gondwana, with older samples reporting no triletes,
younger samples reporting them and counted target and control grains, would show whether supply limits of this size hold outside one polar basin; a fully
reported marine sequence older than 515.5 Ma, with comparable recovery documented, would help quantify
the oldest maximum's absence argument. Finer, independent ages for the Yea (Victoria, Australia) and
Bathurst Island (Arctic Canada) horizons, which hold the oldest possible crown vascular-plant finds,
would sharpen the body-fossil limit [@earp2024NotanopliidBrachiopodsAssociated;
@kotyk2002MorphologicallyComplexPlant], and finer ages generally would show which finds share a bed, and
so how many are independent. Characters placing the oldest branched sporophytes on the
lycophyte or euphyllophyte side of the vascular crown could change which fossils enter the category, as
well as their link to the ancestor.

## Conclusion

A fossil maximum based on absence combines expectations about ancestral characters with the chance of
finding their remains in older rocks. Clock builders can state each maximum's character, geographical
and finding-rate assumptions, and justify the probability assigned to older ages against them
[@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying; @yang2005BayesianEstimationSpecies]. Testing alternative defensible
maxima then shows how much each assumption moves the timescale, and sampling the joint age prior without
molecular data, then comparing its effective marginal ages with the posteriors, shows what the molecular
data add [@brown2017PastSureTense].

For the oldest vascular-plant body fossils, the rule of three makes the finding-rate assumption
concrete: if fossils turned up at a steady rate after the oldest find, the silence before it fits about
three expected finds at that rate.
An older ancestor needs early finding low enough, or recognisable bodies late enough, to keep that
expected count at about three or fewer, and the number of independent finds sets how many million years three finds
span. North American land-laid rock area and the share of studies reporting trilete spores fell enough
before the oldest find to allow older ancestors, but finding that followed them would have left far fewer
fossils in the late Silurian than the at least [13]{.C59 .V207} found there, so they give little reason
to trust that fall. The pooled late-Silurian fossils instead show finding near its later average rate, and they come from
six regions. If the ancestor is older, the simple histories that fit have early finding rise steeply to that
level near the oldest find, which suggests a rapid change whose cause remains open[]{.C60}. For spores, the Libyan basin shows that an absence can be local:
older land-plant ancestors fit its record if, with equal delivery, their steady trilete supply since
459.1 Ma stayed below about a tenth of the later supply, with older onsets needing less, or if the
producers lived elsewhere or acquired triletes later. Missing fossils thus
allow older vascular-plant ancestors if early plants were found less often or became recognisable later,
and the body record states how much less often.

## Disposition

The three fossil cautions have support in the exact cited works, with a repair
needed to distinguish marine preservation from demonstrated transport. The
labelled evolutionary hypothesis is usable, but two arguments need substantive
qualification: an assumed 1% population growth rate does not exclude slow net
demographic growth, and shared inheritance does not date the crown split near
the first fossils. Labelling the paragraph interpretation does not remove those
logical requirements.

## Trigger and demographic logic

**The arithmetic illustration is correct, but its inference is not.** A sustained
1% annual net increase doubles a population in about 70 years. This demonstrates
what that assumed rate would do; it does not estimate early-plant net growth or
put a lower bound on it. Net population growth can approach zero, including
through births and losses nearly balancing or density limitation. Finding also
combines geography, diagnosability, preservation and collection. Therefore a
million-year doubling in finding need not require a cause other than demographic
growth, and the paper cannot use the 1% illustration to rule slow demography out.

A minimal repair keeps it illustrative: at an assumed 1% annual net increase,
population multiplication would be fast on this geological scale, but the
record estimates neither net population growth nor dispersal. Slow net growth,
restricted habitat, dispersal, evolution, preservation and searching could all
pace the rise in finding. No numerical population-growth estimate follows.

**Inheritance orders events but does not measure their separation.** If both
living branches inherited a homologous innovation from their common ancestor,
that innovation precedes their split. This alone puts no bound on how much later
the split, spread, diagnosable crown features or first recovered crown fossil
appeared. A long gap remains possible with a shared ancestral innovation.
Consequently, the present “their ancestor is little older” conclusion does not
follow from shared inheritance alone.

The close-split hypothesis can be retained conditionally: if the shared
innovation promptly made both branches widespread and recognisable as crown
members, and their remains were readily found, the first crown fossils could
follow the split soon. Those are additional assumptions. A later or
environmental trigger also permits an older ancestor, but it is not the only
route to one. Trait ordering can help test the scenario; it does not remove
ecological or observation-process uncertainty.

**Preserve the hypothesis rather than dating a demonstrated switch.** The tests
constrain interval averages and selected rise shapes, while a preservation or
searching change can produce the same record pattern. Thus “a switch would date
spread rather than lineage origin” is defensible only under the biological-spread
interpretation. The general statement should concern a rise in the finding of
recognisable bodies, whose cause is open. The final preservation caution is
therefore necessary and should remain.

Minimal source repair: say that many early fossils are fragmentary and preserved
in marine or near-shore deposits, so their growth habitats and transport routes
are inferred; cite the same works. For the spore caution, name C. barrandei's
in-situ triletes and compare them with the older dispersed trilete record, without
identifying the producers as the same lineage.

# Precedents: what has been modelled about early land plants, and where simulation can add

Compiled 2026-10-26 for the Silurian plants programme (Late Ordovician to Early Devonian, c. 458–393 Ma). Scope: prior modelling and quantitative work, what it showed, how far it was tested, and where a new simulation study could contribute.

**Reading depth.** Substantial parts of the full text were read for Niklas (2004), Porada et al. (2016), Lenton et al. (2016), Dahl et al. (2022), D'Ario et al. (2024), Gibling & Davies (2012), Davies & McMahon (2021) and Raven (2018). Everything else was read at abstract or publisher-summary level, and results relayed through another paper are flagged. Searches for 2018–2026 work were run for every strand, and recent studies that overlap a proposed gap are named in that gap. Years are the conventional volume years.

## Summary

Early-plant modelling is a set of poorly connected traditions: single-organism optimisation (Niklas); organ-scale biophysics of hydraulics and gas exchange; Earth-system box models in which plants enter as prescribed time series; population-genetic life-cycle theory built for algae and mutation load; and statistical treatments of the fossil record. No existing model has populations of plants with explicit physiology that evolve, compete, disperse and feed back on their environment, and are then fitted to dated fossil and proxy data. Several recent studies come close in one respect each: D'Ario et al. (2024) for functional landscapes of sporangia, Dahl et al. (2022) for mechanistic gas exchange coupled to COPSE, Halder et al. (2022) for a dynamic lycopsid vegetation model, Sorojsrisom et al. (2022) for haploid–diploid simulation, and Cai et al. (2026) and Hooper et al. (2026) for new time-resolved targets. None pre-empts the gaps listed at the end; Hooper et al. (2026) sharpens Gaps 3, 5 and 6.

## a. Computational morphospace and adaptive-walk models

**What and how.** Niklas's programme is the only sustained simulation line (Niklas 1982; Niklas & Kerchner 1984; Niklas 1994; 1997a; 1997b; 1999; 2004). A morphospace of bifurcating sporophytes is parameterised by branching probabilities and bifurcation and rotation angles (about 200,000 phenotypes in 1994, about 10^9 by 1999). Each variant is scored for light interception, mechanical stability, spore production and dispersal, and water conservation (minimal surface area), alone or in combination, and hill-climbing walks start from a Cooksonia-like isobifurcate form.

**Results.** The number of fitness peaks rises, and their height above the mean falls, as more tasks are scored at once (Niklas 1994; 1997b). Hypothetical developmental barriers did not reduce the number of optima reached, and abrupt shifts between landscapes increased the number found (Niklas 1997a; 1999). Niklas (2004) reads this as multi-task optimisation accelerating Silurian–Devonian morphological tempo. The earlier simulations showed that trends in branching among early plant groups can be reproduced by random branching with increasing size, so need no deterministic explanation, while the regular-to-geometric transition and canalised reiteration may still reflect selection (Niklas 1982); and that planation and overtopping maximise photosynthetic efficiency or its ratio to bending moment (Niklas & Kerchner 1984).

**Limitations.** Niklas states that the 1999 results follow axiomatically from how variants are ordered, how fitness is defined and how the walk is run, and that the 2004 simulations show logical consequences of assumptions, not tests, because history cannot be manipulated. The water-conservation task returns the Y-shaped starting form trivially (Niklas 2004). Fitness is the performance of an isolated individual: no competition, demography, CO2, explicit hydraulics or gas exchange, and no fit to stratigraphic data. Waller (1984) published a comment on the 1982 branching simulations (read by title only). Searches found no independent re-implementation.

**Successors and adjacent work.** Watson et al. (2008) coupled a gene-regulatory model to Niklas's morphology, offered as a scaffold with no empirical test. Stein & Boyer (2006) replaced the telome theory with an L-system of context-dependent rules (apex strength, allocation at branch points, branch angle, termination) that generates fossil-like architectures but has no fitness function. Chomicki et al. (2017) added 12 Palaeozoic architectural models and found dichotomous branching declining through the Devonian and Carboniferous in 58,927 fossils. Clark et al. (2023) mapped disparity across the plant kingdom: episodic increases separated by reproductive innovations. Hetherington et al. (2021) and El-Abdallah et al. (2026) exemplify evidence-based, quantitative whole-plant reconstruction.

**Open.** A dynamic version with competition, environment and demography, whose predicted order and timing of innovations is fitted to dated first appearances.

## b. Biophysical and physiological models

**Size and physiology.** Boyce (2008) argued that once desiccation resistance, support and transport are costed, small Cooksonia-like axes lack volume for aerated photosynthetic tissue and were probably dependent on an unpreserved gametophyte; "Cooksonia-like" taxa span an order of magnitude in axis width. This is a constraint argument resting on assumed cell sizes and tissue fractions, not a simulation.

**Gas exchange.** Konrad et al. (2000) combined diffusion through a porous medium with a standard photosynthesis model for Aglaophyton: very low transpiration, low but adequate assimilation, saturated even at one-third of the assumed CO2. Roth-Nebelsick & Konrad (2003) extended it to Rhynia and Nothia, ranked gas-exchange capacity Aglaophyton < Rhynia < Nothia, and inferred high Early Devonian CO2 (abstract-level summary). Parameters come from extant plants, models are steady-state, and stomatal function is assumed. Roth & Mosbrugger (1996) read early stele evolution as size-related multi-criteria optimisation of xylem volume and path length.

**Hydraulics.** Wilson & Fischer (2011) built a geometry-constrained resistance model for Asteroxylon: low-resistance tracheids with large scalariform pit areas, no safety features, so likely vulnerable to desiccation; low stomatal density may reflect water limitation rather than high CO2, which weakens stomatal CO2 proxies. Per Raven (2018), Wilson (2016) compared Aglaophyton, Asteroxylon and five Psilophyton species and found plants of high conductivity and low embolism resistance coexisting with plants of lower conductivity and higher resistance around 410–390 Ma. Raven (2018) puts Rhynie xylem conductivity below 1/100 of the best extant values, sporophyte heights at about 200–450 mm, and leaves unresolved whether stomata acted as in seed plants, whether embolism could be repaired and whether desiccation tolerance capped height.

**Stomata and CO2.** Edwards et al. (1998) supplied the Silurian–Devonian stomatal anatomy and frequencies on which these arguments rest. Franks & Beerling (2009a; 2009b) showed with diffusion theory that stomatal size and density must co-vary with CO2 to hold maximum conductance; Franks et al. (2014) turned the leaf gas-exchange model into a CO2 proxy. Beerling et al. (2001) found by energy-balance simulation that a planate leaf with Early Devonian stomatal density would overheat, so megaphylls waited for the Late Palaeozoic CO2 fall; Raven (2002) argued for pre-stomatal pores. Whether early stomata were active is disputed: passive in lycophytes and ferns (Brodribb & McAdam 2011) versus active in Selaginella (Ruszala et al. 2011); phylogenomics places stomata at the base of embryophytes (Harris et al. 2020) and Clark et al. (2022) conclude that hydroactive movement is ancestral. All of this rests on living analogues.

**Mechanics, height and roots.** Mechanics is the thinnest strand. Niklas & Kerchner (1984) treated bending moment; Raven (2018) reports lignin in the outer cortex of Rhynie plants and proposes hydraulics, not support, as the likely limit to height. Searches found no recent quantitative height or buckling model for these axes. Wilson & Knoll (2010) built a physiologically explicit tracheid morphospace (cell length, diameter, pit resistance) for seed plants, a template for Devonian xylem. Raven & Edwards (2001) put roots' origin in at least two Early Devonian lineages; Hetherington & Dolan (2018) found Asteroxylon rooting axes with meristems but no root cap; Hao et al. (2010) estimated a root:shoot ratio of 0.028 for a 98 mm Zosterophyllum.

**Reproduction and mechanics.** D'Ario et al. (2024) reconstructed 15 charcoalified Lochkovian sporangia (Brown Clee, Shropshire) in 3D from SEM images and ran finite-element inflation: rupture at about 0.17–0.4 MPa, four morphological groups, and a landscape of spore yield (cavity volume), rupture pressure (a dispersal proxy) and stress-assisted rupture, with no specimen near the generalist centre. Limits they state: constant material properties and wall thickness, tensile strength borrowed from leaf and onion epidermis, no fluid dynamics, no environment in the fitness.

**Open.** Everything is organ-scale and steady-state. Nothing integrates hydraulics, gas exchange, mechanics and spore dispersal across a changing CO2, and nothing evolves.

## c. Earth-system and biogeochemical models

**Lineage.** The Devonian Plant Hypothesis (Berner 1997; Retallack 1997; Algeo & Scheckler 1998) tied deep-rooted trees to weathering, soils, CO2 fall, marine eutrophication and anoxia. Box models (COPSE: Bergman 2004; Lenton et al. 2018; GEOCARBSULF: Berner 2006, revised by Krause et al. 2018 and Mills et al. 2019) carry plants as time-dependent scalings of weathering, burial and fire. Beerling & Berner (2005) mapped positive plant–CO2 feedbacks. Le Hir et al. (2011) coupled climate, carbon and vegetation: CO2 fell from 6300 to 2100 ppmv through the Devonian but albedo loss held temperature roughly constant; Brugger et al. (2019) found the biogeophysical vegetation effect small and unable to explain the Late Devonian temperature rise. Taylor et al. (2012) built a process-based weathering scheme but applied it only from 215 to 50 Ma.

**The pre-vascular phase.** Lenton et al. (2012; full text not accessible, so details come via Porada et al. 2016 and secondary summaries) combined moss-on-rock microcosms with a carbon-cycle model, reportedly with non-vascular plants covering about 15% of vegetated land. Ca and Mg weathering were amplified by about 1.5–5 times and iron by over two orders of magnitude, from one moss species under modern air. Porada et al. (2016) used a global process-based cryptogam model (Porada et al. 2013) with 300 initial artificial species on a 450 Ma climate: NPP about 25% of modern, weathering 2.8 km^3 rock per year (about three times today's), strongly CO2-sensitive; a minimum-sensitivity run gave 1.6. Lenton et al. (2016) ran a trait-based cryptogam model at 4, 8 and 16 PAL CO2 (about 19 GtC per year at 8 PAL, 30% of modern), then prescribed in COPSE a colonisation factor rising to 0.15 over 465–445 Ma, a C:P of 1000–2000, raised weathering and selective P release. Result: δ13C up 2.2‰ and O2 at modern levels by 420–400 Ma. Their caveats: C:P rests on unmeasured biomass P, P-weathering pulses are hypothesised, and δ34S fits imperfectly.

**Critiques and updates.** Quirk et al. (2015) found liverworts with AM fungi raised Ca weathering 3–7 times but shallow-anchored early plants were at least 10-fold less effective than trees. D'Antonio et al. (2020) argued from carbon mass balance that organic-carbon burial implies lower, not higher, silicate weathering rates. Field et al. (2012) found liverwort AM efficiency falling at low CO2 while vascular sporophytes' rose; Mills et al. (2018) showed in a box model that fungal nutrient-acquisition strategy could greatly alter plant-driven climate change. Halder et al. (2022) built a dynamic local lycopsid model, run only under modern climate. Dahl et al. (2022) calibrated a mechanistic gas-exchange model on living Lycopodiaceae, applied it to 66 fossil lycophyte specimens, and obtained 525–715 ppm CO2 at 410–380 Ma (±100 ppm); in COPSE, shallow-rooted vascular ecosystems could have produced the oxygenation and cooling, with trees adding little. Smart et al. (2023) drove COPSE with Devonian lake P records to test a Late Devonian anoxia link, and Ibarra et al. (2019) added transpiration-driven inland moisture recycling to weathering models. Davies & McMahon (2021) and Dahl & Arens (2021) disagree on what the mudrock record implies for erosion; Dahl & Arens (2020) conclude that how each transition affected O2 and CO2 remains debated.

**New constraints.** Cai et al. (2026) find a Corg/Ptotal shift from about 455 Ma; Yuan et al. (2023) Hg-isotope excursions from the early Silurian, implying vascular plants before Cooksonia; Avila & Saltzman (2025) a Sr and δ18O inflection in the late Silurian–Early Devonian; Wu et al. (2026) a Li-isotope weathering pulse in the early Pragian.

**Limitations.** Plants enter as prescribed forcings; cryptogam cover is an equilibrium potential without dispersal or evolution; the sign and size of plant effects on weathering are contested; and most models are confronted with proxies of low temporal resolution.

## d. Life-cycle theory

**Haploid–diploid models.** Mable & Otto (1998) reviewed theory. Hughes & Otto (1999) showed that ecological differences between phases, in a density-dependent genetic model parameterised on the red alga Gracilaria, can maintain biphasic cycles. Later work added meiotic mutation (Hall 2000), intrinsic fitness differences (Scott & Rescan 2017) and interacting genetic and ecological effects with evolutionary branching (Rescan et al. 2016). These models treat the life-cycle modifier locus abstractly; none has dispersal, desiccation, matrotrophy or sporophyte size.

**Origin of the sporophyte.** Haig (2008) argued that modern evidence favours an updated antithetic theory. Hemsley (1994) and Kato & Akiyama (2005) developed interpolation scenarios; Niklas & Kutschera (2010) argued for a haplobiontic ancestor and a dimorphic cycle even in Cooksonia, Rhynia and Zosterophyllum. Fossils contradict the last point: Rhynie gametophytes are axial and more or less isomorphic with sporophytes (Remy et al. 1993; Kenrick 1994; Taylor et al. 2005; Gerrienne & Gonez 2011). Kenrick (2018) proposed that independence of the sporophyte arose by expanding gametophytic gene expression into it, via an extinct isomorphic variant. Bowman et al. (2016) review the genetics of the transition.

**Optimality and conflict models.** Haig & Wilczek (2006) predicted sexual conflict over allocation to sporophytes, strongest in bryophytes; Haig (2015) showed zygotic division becoming favoured above a size threshold in Coleochaete; Bessho & Sasaki (2024) modelled maternal investment, with parental conflict driving escalation. Sorojsrisom et al. (2022) built shadie, a SLiM-based simulator of biphasic life cycles, so far applied to genome diversity in generic modern life cycles. For spore size, Haig & Westoby (1988) modelled heterospory; Petersen & Burd (2017) review resource-allocation arguments; Petersen & Burd (2018) found in 114 Selaginella species that spore sizes track canopy density, consistent with heterospory as a response to Devonian canopies.

**Limits and open.** Theory is abstract and untested against the fossil constraints. Open: an ecological model of sporophyte elaboration and independence with dispersal, water-limited fertilisation and size.

## e. Macroevolutionary and diversification analyses

**Diversity curves.** Knoll et al. (1984) found the main early tracheophyte diversification in the late Early to early Middle Devonian, about 30 Myr after origin, with mosaic organ-level evolution. Raymond & Metz (1995) showed that all diversity measures are influenced by the number of floras sampled. Capel et al. (2021) resolved four successive floras by factor analysis; Cascales-Miñana & Meyer-Berthaud (2014) used locality counts to show that sampling distorts zosterophyll diversity little in the Early Devonian but strongly later; Capel et al. (2022) found stepwise origination and extinction, with observed diversity strongly correlated with locality count, and sampling-corrected increases in the Pragian and Givetian; Capel et al. (2023) found North American diversity tracks sedimentary rock volume, especially non-marine, and that the lack of pre-Emsian terrestrial deposits blurs the early radiation. Silvestro et al. (2015) used a Bayesian birth–death-with-preservation approach (PyRate) on macrofossil occurrences: shifts in origination and extinction often matching boundaries, and clade origins predating macrofossils.

**Spores.** Steemans et al. (2009) reported trilete spores from the Late Ordovician. Cascales-Miñana (2016) combined spores with megafossils: spore diversity rises steadily with an early late-Silurian maximum, whereas megafossil peaks fall in the Pragian, Givetian and Visean (HAL abstract). Servais et al. (2019) and Wellman et al. (2023) review the Ordovician record. Pšenička et al. (2021) and Bek et al. (2024) correlate spore richness with glaciation and climate belts, descriptively. Edwards et al. (2022) place permanent-cryptospore producers in a new group, the eophytes. Garza et al. (2024) dated Cooksonia-bearing siltstones in Ireland and Wales by detrital zircon U–Pb, giving maximum depositional ages of 423–427 Ma, as inputs for future calibrations.

**Clocks and traits.** Morris et al. (2018) integrated 37 fossil calibrations and recovered a Cambrian origin of embryophytes and Late Ordovician to late Silurian crown tracheophytes. Hooper et al. (2026), reconstructing spore character evolution (their fossil sample is 11 megafossil and 12 dispersed-spore taxa), infer that the crown embryophyte already made trilete spores, so cryptospores, which belong to the stem, no longer calibrate the crown. Node calibrations are uniform between minimum and maximum bounds with 2.5% tail probabilities; the result is crown embryophytes at 457–450 Ma, crown tracheophytes at 432–428 Ma and crown bryophytes at 445–429 Ma. The embryophyte crown has therefore moved from the Cambrian to the Late Ordovician within eight years. They note that the spore record is more continuous than the megafossil record and precedes Cooksonia by about 40 Myr. Heath et al. (2014) supply the fossilised birth–death framework; searches found no fossilised birth–death application to Siluro-Devonian tracheophytes. Trait studies include Boyce & Knoll (2002) on leaf morphospace, Hetherington & Dolan (2018) on convergent roots and Harrison & Morris (2018) on shoots and leaves.

**Limits.** Curves count taxa, not rates; sampling proxies are crude; clock results depend on topology, calibrations and the crown or stem affinity of spores. Searches found no study that uses simulation or generative sampling models (virtual palaeontology, simulated preservation) to put confidence intervals on early land-plant first appearances. The nearest are the preservation-aware origin estimates of Silvestro et al. (2015), Lloyd-type sampling corrections (Cascales-Miñana & Meyer-Berthaud 2014) and the soft calibration bounds of Morris et al. (2018) and Hooper et al. (2026).

## f. Landscape and soil feedbacks

**Fluvial record.** Davies & Gibling (2010) found lateral accretion sets first in the Pridoli–Lochkovian and in about 40% of case studies by the Famennian, matching rooted vegetation, but noted meandering without plants and a surprising absence of Cambrian–Silurian point bars. Gibling & Davies (2012) synthesised roots, banks and woody debris. McMahon & Davies (2018) used 704 alluvial formations to show alluvium after land plants has a mudrock fraction 1.4 orders of magnitude higher, onset Ordovician–Silurian. McMahon et al. (2023) argued the increase persists in unvegetated channel-bar facies, so plants raised mud production, not only retention; Davies & McMahon (2021) and Dahl & Arens (2021) dispute the erosion inference.

**Models and tests.** Lapôtre et al. (2019) used open-channel hydraulics and a bank-erosion model to show that muddy cohesive banks alone can give single-thread rivers without plants. Ielpi & Lapôtre (2020) found barren-bank meanders migrate about ten times faster than vegetated ones across 41 rivers. Hasson et al. (2023) described modern unvegetated meanders with sand-and-mud-aggregate point bars; Finotello et al. (2024) found vegetation raises curvature-driven migration at low curvature, but causality cannot be separated from avulsion frequency.

**Soils.** Retallack (1997) and Xue et al. (2023) document Devonian soils and rhizome traces in drylands; Mitchell et al. (2016; 2023) imaged modern cryptogamic soils as analogues of Rhynie communities. Weathering links are in c.

**Open.** No dynamic coupling of plant traits to a fluvial or hillslope model; the production-versus-retention question remains unresolved.

## g. Transferable eco-evolutionary plant models

**Height and light competition.** Falster & Westoby (2003) framed height as an evolutionary game under light competition. Falster et al. (2011) built a trait-, size- and patch-structured vegetation model with a physiological growth model and trade-offs; Falster et al. (2016) released it as the plant package; Falster et al. (2017) added evolutionary community assembly and found fitness equivalence emerging among shade-tolerant strategies. Scheiter et al. (2013) built aDGVM2, an individual- and trait-based model with a genetic algorithm; Fisher et al. (2018) review demographic vegetation models.

**Architecture and artificial life.** Bornhofen & Lattaud (2009) and Bornhofen et al. (2011) evolve L-system plants with explicit physiology and demography and recover competitor, stress-tolerator and ruderal strategies. Prusinkiewicz & Runions (2012) and Louarn & Song (2020) review the developmental and functional–structural models that supply geometry and light engines.

**What transfers.** The patch-structured invasion-fitness machinery, the L-system and light-interception code, and cryptogam and lycopsid process modules (Porada et al. 2013; Halder et al. 2022). **What does not.** Leaf-area and wood-density allometry, self-thinning, seed banks and canopy gap dynamics; early plants lack leaves, grow indeterminately by dichotomy, are partly poikilohydric, have a free-living gametophyte, and disperse spores.

## Gaps and opportunities

Each gap lists (i) why it is open, (ii) the kind of model, (iii) the data to confront it, and (iv) the closest precedent and how a new study differs. Gaps 1, 4 and 6 are the most promising and novel; Gaps 2, 3 and 7 are strong; Gaps 5, 8 and 9 are the most data-limited.

**1. What eco-evolutionary dynamics drove height and axis-system escalation among leafless axes?**

*(i)* Height games have only been built for leafy trees, and Niklas's walks have no frequency dependence. Rhynie sporophytes were about 200–450 mm (Raven 2018) and size and physiology are coupled (Boyce 2008), yet no study asks what selected for taller leafless axes when competitors set the optimum. *(ii)* A trait- and patch-structured individual-based model in the plant package tradition: axes as photosynthetic, hydraulic and supporting units, with conductance and safety after Wilson & Fischer (2011) and Roth & Mosbrugger (1996), gas exchange after Konrad et al. (2000) and Franks & Beerling (2009a), height-dependent shading and spore dispersal. Evolve diameter, branching, tissue fractions and stomatal density under disturbance and a CO2 path, and ask whether escalation is gradual or switch-like and whether hydraulic or mechanical limits bind first. *(iii)* Size ranges at Rhynie, the ordering of height classes in the Capel et al. (2022) genus data, CO2 from Dahl et al. (2022) and Franks et al. (2014). *(iv)* Closest: Falster et al. (2017) and Niklas (2004). New: frequency-dependent selection on early-plant physiology, tested on fossil size data.

**2. Can a developmental–functional morphospace with dynamics reproduce the order and tempo of architectural innovations?**

*(i)* Niklas's results are axiomatic, Stein & Boyer (2006) have no fitness, and D'Ario et al. (2024) score sporangia only, in one Lochkovian flora, using proxies for dispersal and yield and constant material properties. *(ii)* L-system genotype-to-phenotype maps, multi-objective performance (light, hydraulics, mechanics, gas exchange against CO2, spore yield and release), mutation and drift with a CO2 path. *(iii)* Chomicki et al. (2017) branching frequencies through time, disparity in Clark et al. (2023), first appearances in Capel et al. (2022), quantitative reconstructions such as Hetherington et al. (2021) and El-Abdallah et al. (2026). *(iv)* Closest: Niklas (1999) and D'Ario et al. (2024). New: dynamics, whole-plant function and fitting.

**3. How fast and how widely did land cover spread in the Ordovician–Silurian, given the proxies?**

*(i)* Lenton et al. (2016) prescribed the colonisation factor and Porada et al. (2016) computed equilibrium potentials. The clock has since moved (Morris et al. 2018; Hooper et al. 2026), new targets exist (Cai et al. 2026; Yuan et al. 2023), and they pull in different directions: Yuan et al. (2023) infer widespread vascular plants by about 444 Ma, whereas Hooper et al. (2026) place crown tracheophytes at 432–428 Ma, so the stem versus crown meaning of "vascular" matters. *(ii)* A spatially explicit colonisation and evolution model on palaeogeographic grids: dispersal kernels, NPP-based suitability (Porada et al. 2013), poikilohydric trait evolution, disturbance, feeding burial and weathering into a COPSE-type model; infer parameters by approximate Bayesian computation, with the Hooper et al. (2026) node ages as priors. *(iii)* Cai et al. (2026) C/P, Yuan et al. (2023) Hg, δ13C and O2 targets in Lenton et al. (2016), Steemans et al. (2009) and Wellman et al. (2023) spore records. *(iv)* Closest: Lenton et al. (2016). Hooper et al. (2026) sharpens the question and the priors but does not pre-empt it, since it models neither cover nor the Earth system. New: dynamic, spatial and inverse.

**4. Do evolving plant traits close the carbon, oxygen and phosphorus budget, and what does that say about the Devonian Plant Hypothesis?**

*(i)* All Earth-system treatments prescribe plant effects. Dahl et al. (2022) find trees added little, Quirk et al. (2015) infer trees were at least tenfold more effective than shallow-rooted early plants, and D'Antonio et al. (2020) argue weathering rates fell; the prescribed forcings cannot arbitrate. *(ii)* Replace COPSE's prescribed colonisation, C:P and weathering factors with outputs of an eco-evolutionary module whose evolving variables are rooting depth, biomass C:P, litter decay resistance, P-weathering effort and fungal strategy, with fitness from net carbon gain given P and water, and CO2 acting on stomata and selection. Ask which trait trajectories satisfy the carbon mass balance and the proxies simultaneously. *(iii)* CO2 (Dahl et al. 2022; Foster et al. 2017), charcoal O2 (Glasspool & Scott 2010), Sr and δ18O (Avila & Saltzman 2025), palaeosols (Wu et al. 2026), mudrock (McMahon & Davies 2018), lake P (Smart et al. 2023). *(iv)* Closest: Mills et al. (2018), Dahl et al. (2022) and Beerling & Berner (2005). New: traits are endogenous, so the forcings become outputs.

**5. When do independent, elaborated sporophytes evolve under dispersal and desiccation constraints?**

*(i)* Existing models are genetic or optimality models without dispersal, water-limited fertilisation or size; Rhynie gametophytes were isomorphic (Taylor et al. 2005) and Boyce (2008) implies dependent small sporophytes. *(ii)* Stage-structured, spatially explicit demographic model with matrotrophy–independence trade-offs and spore output. *(iii)* Gerrienne & Gonez (2011) size data, Kenrick (2018) transitions, spore character evolution (monoplastidic meiosis, trilete tetrads at the crown) in Hooper et al. (2026). *(iv)* Closest: Hughes & Otto (1999), Bessho & Sasaki (2024), Sorojsrisom et al. (2022). New: ecology and morphology, not mutation load.

**6. How much of the Silurian–Devonian radiation, and how much of the apparent timing of first appearances, is the rock and preservation record?**

*(i)* Capel et al. (2022; 2023) show that diversity correlates with localities and rock volume but do not simulate the observation process; Silvestro et al. (2015) infer preservation rather than generate it; and, as far as searches show, no study gives simulation-based confidence intervals on first appearances of early land plants. Hooper et al. (2026) sharpens the need: spores and megafossils now carry different weight in the clock, with a roughly 40 Myr offset between the spore record and Cooksonia, and spore affinity (crown or stem) is itself uncertain. *(ii)* A forward generative model: trait-dependent birth–death, habitat capacity from non-marine rock volume (as in Capel et al. 2023), preservation by facies and mode (compression, charcoal, permineralised, dispersed spore), sampling effort, and a latent affinity layer for spore morphotypes. Fit by approximate Bayesian computation to genus ranges and stage richness. Outputs: a posterior over true standing diversity, a test of whether the Pragian rise survives, the share of apparent turnover due to preservation, and conditional intervals on the gap between true origin and first appearance in each channel. *(iii)* Occurrences from PBDB and the Capel et al. compilations (if released); spore richness (Cascales-Miñana 2016; Ball 2022) as an independent second channel; the maximum ages of Garza et al. (2024) for Cooksonia sites. *(iv)* Closest: Capel et al. (2023), Silvestro et al. (2015). New: generative, joint across two data streams, and aimed at first-appearance intervals.

**7. Did early plants muddy rivers by producing or retaining fine sediment?**

*(i)* McMahon et al. (2023) argue for production; Lapôtre et al. (2019) show muddy banks alone can fix channels; Dahl & Arens (2021) disagree. *(ii)* Reduced-complexity hillslope and channel model with vegetation cover, rooting and baffling as state variables from a plant-dynamics module. *(iii)* McMahon & Davies (2018), Davies & Gibling (2010), Ielpi & Lapôtre (2020), Mitchell et al. (2023). *(iv)* Closest: Lapôtre et al. (2019). New: dynamic vegetation, tested on mudrock fractions.

**8. What selected for rooting axes and fungal partnerships as CO2 fell?**

*(i)* Experiments (Field et al. 2012; Quirk et al. 2015) and box models (Mills et al. 2018) exist; searches found no model of rooting evolution. *(ii)* Adaptive-dynamics model of root:shoot allocation, P uptake via AM fungi and water, under CO2 decline. *(iii)* Hao et al. (2010) root:shoot ratio, Hetherington & Dolan (2018), Xue et al. (2023). *(iv)* Closest: Mills et al. (2018). New: evolving allocation.

**9. How vulnerable were early land plant populations to Silurian cooling, and how did they recover?**

*(i)* Pšenička et al. (2021) and Bek et al. (2024) correlate richness with climate descriptively; Avila & Saltzman (2025) report an 8–10 °C late Silurian–Early Devonian cooling event. *(ii)* A stochastic metapopulation model with a gametophyte bottleneck and climate-dependent niches, fitted by approximate Bayesian computation. *(iii)* Spore richness by climate belt, glaciation events, Dahl et al.

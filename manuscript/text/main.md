# When did living land plants arise? What their fossil age limits require

<div id="authors"></div>

## Abstract

Molecular clocks date the ancestor of living land plants from the Cambrian to the Late
Ordovician, partly through different fossil maximum ages. We examined three maxima:
515.5 and 459.1 million years ago (Ma) for living land plants, and 431.2 Ma for living vascular plants
(clubmosses, ferns and seed plants). We traced their sources, modelled Libyan spore records
and set a limit on when the vascular-plant body-fossil record began. The oldest
rests on a qualitative preservation argument. Libyan boreholes yield three-rayed
(trilete) spores in [51 of 59]{.C02} early Silurian samples and none of [28]{.C01} older ones.
If spores reached the rocks equally easily throughout, steady supply since 459.1 Ma is limited to
[9]{.C24}% of the later supply (95% limit). If vascular-plant fossils were found at a steady rate, the
95% confidence limit on their record's start is [428.8]{.C27 .V003} Ma; counting fewer independent finds
gives [432.4–434.05]{.C30} Ma. Ancestors at 444–452 Ma fit if fossils were found
[8]{.C48 .V054}–[12]{.C48 .V055} times less often before the oldest find than after it
([2.4]{.C48 .V056}–[4.5]{.C48 .V057} with fewer finds), with recognisable bodies appearing
with the ancestor. Older dates for living land plants fit the fossil record if early plants were found
several times less often, or recognisable features evolved later.

**Keywords:** molecular clock; fossil calibration; land plants; vascular plants; stratigraphic
confidence interval; Ordovician–Silurian

## Introduction

Molecular clocks estimate when lineages diverged by combining DNA differences with fossil limits on the
ages of selected ancestors. A crown group comprises the most recent ancestor shared by its living
members and all its descendants, including extinct lineages. For the ancestor of all living land plants
(crown embryophytes), Morris et al. [@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand] obtained 515–474 Ma across seven
tree topologies and Harris et al. [@harris2022DivergentEvolutionaryTrajectories] 515–493 Ma. Hooper et
al. [@hooper2026ReconstructingSporeEvolution] reanalysed Harris et al.'s DNA data with revised fossil
calibrations and obtained 457–450 Ma, a mean 55 Myr younger. For the ancestor of living vascular plants
(crown tracheophytes: clubmosses and their relatives, ferns and seed plants), the three studies give
451–419, 452–447 and 432–428 Ma (figure 1); Su et al. [@su2021LargescalePhylogenomicAnalyses] obtained
older land-plant ages still, under a Proterozoic maximum. The dates bear on the order in which plant
characters were assembled, and on how early plants changed productivity, weathering and climate
[@kenrick2012TimelineTerrestrializationConsequences; @donoghue2021EvolutionaryEmergenceLand;
@strother2021FossilRecordLand; @lenton2012FirstPlantsCooled]. They date the ancestors of living plants;
extinct lineages may have reached land earlier.

A fossil sets a minimum age: its lineage existed by the age of its oldest secure member. A maximum
based on absence adds an argument that older rocks would probably have preserved and yielded
recognisable remains had the lineage existed [@benton2006PaleontologicalEvidenceDate]. How calibrations
are specified can strongly shape estimated dates, and with large sequence datasets they can dominate
the uncertainty in absolute ages [@ho2009AccountingCalibrationUncertainty;
@dosreis2015BayesianMolecularClock]. A soft maximum permits older ages through a specified tail of the
calibration prior [@yang2005BayesianEstimationSpecies]. Whether absence can set a maximum is contested
[@hedges2018AccurateTimetreesRequire; @morris2018ReplyHedgesEt], and best practice asks each calibration
to justify its maximum from the relevant geological, geographical and taphonomic record
[@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying].

Linking a fossil to an ancestor crosses two intervals: from divergence to the first recognisable
character, and from that character to its first recorded fossil [@marshall2019UsingFossilRecord]. Both
are uncertain for early land plants. Dispersed spores appear tens of millions of years before branched
plant bodies [@wellman2000MicrofossilRecordEarly; @rubinstein2010EarlyMiddleOrdovician;
@steemans2009OriginRadiationEarliest], terrestrial deposits are scarce before the latest Silurian, at
least in western Europe [@kenrick2012TimelineTerrestrializationConsequences], and the oldest parent
plants known in detail are minute [@edwards2014CryptosporesCryptophytesReveal].

Three maxima divide the current clocks (table 1). Morris et al. set 515.5 Ma for living land plants,
arguing that older marine rocks rich in algal cysts would also have yielded land-plant spores
[@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand; @morris2018ReplyHedgesEt]. Hooper et al. set 459.1 Ma from the oldest
possible trilete (three-rayed) spores, in the Borenshult-1 core of southern Sweden
[@rubinstein2019BalticaCradleEarly], and 431.2 Ma for living vascular plants from Irish *Cooksonia*
[@edwards1980RecordsCooksoniatypeSporangia]. The three therefore rest on a preservation analogy, on
dispersed spores and on plant bodies, each with its own route into the record, and each needs its own
test.

We ask three questions of the published record. What evidence supports each maximum, and what links
connect it to the dated ancestor? How much steady spore supply before the Silurian do the records of one
Libyan basin allow? How much less often would early vascular-plant fossils have to be found, or how long
after the ancestor would recognisable bodies have to appear, for older dates to fit? Established theory
sets an expectation for each. An argument from absence needs documented chances for remains to be
delivered, preserved and found [@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying; @marshall2019UsingFossilRecord].
Where older and younger rocks were sampled comparably, each further sample reporting no target spores
tightens the limit on a high, steady spore supply. With the oldest find and the time window fixed, more independent finds bring
the limit on a record's start closer to that find, and a lower finding rate before it pushes the limit
back [@strauss1989ClassicalConfidenceIntervals; @marshall1990ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic]. The
audit identifies what each maximum assumes; the models limit local spore supply and describe the finding
rates or delays that would fit older vascular-plant dates.

## Material and methods

**(a) Timescale and interpretations.** Analysed ages use the stage bases of the International
Chronostratigraphic Chart, v2024/12 [@cohen2013IcsInternationalChronostratigraphic]; literature ages
were mapped by stage. Published maxima keep their source timescale, GTS2012, so they can be compared as
their authors set them. Each analysis declares which interpretation of disputed identifications it
uses, main or inclusive, so contested fossils enter as alternatives.

**(b) Source audit.** For each maximum we traced the calibration fossil, its age rule and how it was
found back to the original descriptions, and recorded each published interpretation of the fossil's
identity with its evidence [@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying]. A reported absence applies to the
material examined, and a missing report counts as an absence where the source reports the target fully.

**(c) Libyan spore records.** Abuhmida [@abuhmida2013PalynologicalAnalysisOrdovician] reported spores
and other microfossils from Ordovician to lower Silurian wells in the Murzuq Basin, southwest Libya. We
transcribed all [164]{.C38} appendix samples with their chart and prose reports. The analysis uses the
chart-coded samples of drill cuttings (rock chips returned during drilling) from five wells: three supply
[28]{.C01} samples older than the Llandovery (earliest Silurian), and all five supply [59]{.C02}
Llandovery samples, which serve as the reference. The source states the absence of trilete spores
explicitly for part of the sequence; for the Hawaz Formation, samples reporting none rest on complete reporting
charts and the stated microscopy protocol. The basin lay near the Ordovician South Pole, within the area
of Hirnantian glaciation [@cocks2021OrdovicianPalaeogeographyClimate;
@scotese2021PhanerozoicPaleotemperaturesEarths] (figure 3*a*).

**(d) Libyan spore model.** We modelled whether trilete spores were reported in each sample. Spore supply before
the Llandovery is a fraction *f* of Llandovery supply, and a fixed delivery ratio $\rho$ covers
differences between older and younger rocks in transport, preservation and preparation ($\rho=1$:
spores reached both equally easily). Samples from one well share a well effect, gamma-distributed with
mean one (shape and rate 2), and are otherwise independent; each sample's age is uniform within its
stage range. Fitting the Llandovery rate and *f* together, we calculated upper 95% limits on *f* for
steady supply from a given start, and for supply that varied between zero and the Llandovery
level, averaged with each interval weighted by how much of the older samples' age ranges falls within it. Using the younger reports as the
reference, we first checked that the design could exclude steady supply at the Llandovery level if
every older sample reported no triletes and fell within the period of supply; each simulated dataset
faced the same check. We then tested the method on simulated data with known answers. Among simulated
datasets passing that check, the method had either to withhold a limit or to give one at or above the
true value; a quantity was used only if this held in more than 93% of trials with 95% confidence, with a
preplanned pooled rule for borderline cases (electronic supplementary material, S2).

**(e) Body-fossil finds.** We assembled land-plant body fossils dated to 453–393 Ma from Paleobiology
Database (PBDB) occurrences, whose names an AI model classified with cited sources before our audit, and
from a source-reviewed set of the oldest records with their competing interpretations. Collections, taxa
or papers sharing a locality and age count as one find, a proxy for an independent fossil horizon. We
declare three nested categories. Land-plant bodies are any accepted macro- or mesofossil.
Polysporangiophytes have documented branched axes bearing sporangia (spore capsules), or placement
within the vascular crown. Crown vascular plants belong to the lycophyte line (clubmosses and relatives)
or the euphyllophytes (ferns and seed plants) [@kotyk2002MorphologicallyComplexPlant;
@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand; @earp2024NotanopliidBrachiopodsAssociated;
@libertin2024GenusAberlemniaSiluriandevonian]. Under the main interpretation the three categories hold
[78]{.C25}, [71]{.C25} and [50]{.C25} finds, of which [76]{.C40 .V021}, [69]{.C25} and [48]{.C25} are
certainly older than the end of the Emsian ([393.47]{.C25} Ma), the younger edge of the time window
(electronic supplementary material, S5.1).

**(f) Limit on when the record began.** Let *w*(*s*) be a fixed relative rate at which recognisable
fossils of a category are found at age *s*, and $U(a)=\int_c^a w(s)\,ds$ the cumulative finding rate
between a younger cutoff *c*, here the end of the Emsian, and age *a*. For *n* independent finds with
oldest age *A*, the 95% confidence limit *T* on the start of the recognisable record
solves $U(T)=U(A)\,0.05^{-1/n}$ (figure 2). With constant *w* this is the classical confidence interval
on a stratigraphic range, and the absolute rate cancels [@strauss1989ClassicalConfidenceIntervals;
@marshall1990ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic]; varying *w* follows the extensions to unevenly spaced
fossil horizons [@marshall1994ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic;
@marshall1997ConfidenceIntervalsStratigraphic]. For finds dated by age ranges, *A* is the oldest
possible age of the oldest find, and *n* counts only finds certainly inside the window; both choices
move the limit older. The limit concerns the start of the record; the Discussion treats its link to
ancestors.

**(g) Finding-rate histories and shape tests.** We compared six assumed histories of the finding rate:
steady; proportional to PBDB land and marginal-marine collections per Myr
[@brook2026FrozenPaleobiologyDatabase], with or without the collections that hold plant fossils;
declining exponentially into the past with 25 or 10 Myr timescales; and plant-excluded collection
density multiplied by the 10 Myr decline. The collection records reach back to [469.4]{.C27 .V007} Ma,
the oldest age the analysis can consider. For each history, an exact Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compared
the spread of fossil ages it predicts with the observed spread, given the count and the oldest find.
Because many ages are known only as ranges, we searched every possible assignment of ages within those
ranges, including whether the finds straddling the cutoff fall inside the window, for the best fit
[compare @berger1994PValuesMaximized]. A history that fails under every assignment is rejected
(electronic supplementary material, S5.2).

**(h) Lower earlier finding rates and delays.** The main scenario holds the finding rate steady after
[426.7]{.C32} Ma, the oldest possible age of a crown vascular-plant find, and lowers it before that
age. For a candidate ancestor age *t* and an assumed delay *d* before the first recognisable bodies, the
ratio of earlier to later finding rate that puts *t* − *d* where the limit of (*f*) would fall is
$r=(A-c)(0.05^{-1/n}-1)/(t-d-A)$, defined where $t-d>A$; fossils would then have to be found $1/r$ times
less often before the oldest find than after it. Longer delays reduce the reduction a given ancestor
age needs [@marshall2019UsingFossilRecord]. We chose where to put the step after seeing the data, so
these curves show what would fit, without the 95% confidence that (*f*) gives each fixed history.

## Results

**(a) The 515.5 Ma maximum rests on a qualitative argument.** Morris et al. placed this maximum below the
oldest possible total-group records, spore-like packets and clusters from the Cambrian Stage 4 Rome
Formation, at the base of the *Olenellus* Biozone (about [515.5]{.C46} Ma). Their preservation argument,
from older marine rocks with algal cysts and without land-plant spores, shows that walled microfossils
survived and were recovered [@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand; @morris2018ReplyHedgesEt]. How many spores
reached those basins, the land areas they drained and the search effort remain unmeasured in the sources
we assessed[]{.C16}. All [three]{.C15} sources we examined assign the Cambrian packets to algae or
lineages before land plants: Charophyta [@strother2016SystematicsEvolutionarySignificance], a
pre-embryophytic grade [@taylor2024UltrastructureCambrianCryptospores] or ancestral aeroterrestrial
algae [@wang2022PalynologicalAssemblageCambrian] (electronic supplementary material, S4).

**(b) The younger maxima follow a stage-base rule.** Hooper et al. set both younger maxima as a GTS2012
stage base plus its uncertainty. They keyed 459.1 Ma ([458.4 + 0.7 = 459.1]{.C42} Ma) to a possible
trilete spore at 48.2 m in the Borenshult-1 core [@rubinstein2019BalticaCradleEarly]. The oldest
definite trilete level, at 43.1 m, is also Sandbian and lies below the [454.41]{.C43} Ma Kinnekulle
K-bentonite, so the rule gives the same value from either level (electronic supplementary material,
S2.4). They keyed 431.2 Ma ([430.5 + 0.7 = 431.2]{.C47} Ma) to Irish Homerian *Cooksonia*
[@edwards1980RecordsCooksoniatypeSporangia]. The older Czech *Cooksonia barrandei*, a branched
sporophyte with terminal sporangia from a Sheinwoodian (early Silurian) horizon dated from its slab fauna
and by correlation [@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand], meets the same criterion (electronic
supplementary material, table S13). On the current timescale the rule gives outer values of
[431.9]{.C47} Ma for the Homerian and [434.1]{.C47} Ma for the Sheinwoodian.

**(c) Older Libyan samples reporting no triletes limit a steady, well-delivered spore supply.** Trilete spores are reported in
[51 of 59]{.C02} Llandovery samples and in none of the [28]{.C01} older samples (figure 3*a*). If
spores reached both sets of rocks equally easily, a steady supply since 459.1 Ma is limited to
[0.091]{.C24} of the Llandovery supply, and a supply varying between zero and the Llandovery level to an average of [0.184]{.C24} (figure
3*b*; electronic supplementary material, table S12). If older spores reached the rocks a tenth as
easily, steady supply near the Llandovery level fits (limit [0.910]{.C24}). For steady supply the
samples limit the product $\rho f$, which stays at [0.091]{.C24} whatever the delivery.

**(d) The Libyan limits cover mainly the later Ordovician.** Most of the older samples' weight comes
from [18]{.C45} Katian–Hirnantian (Late Ordovician) samples, all younger than 459.1 Ma. Only [10]{.C45}
broadly dated Darriwilian (Middle Ordovician) samples, from two wells, have age ranges reaching beyond
459.1 Ma. Their contribution under a start at that age depends on how ages are spread within the stage;
placed at stage midpoints, they contribute nothing[]{.C09}. The limits therefore concern steady supply
through the later Ordovician in one high-latitude basin.

**(e) At a steady finding rate, the 95% confidence limit on the vascular-plant body record's start is
near 429 Ma.** If crown vascular-plant fossils were found at a steady rate, the [48]{.C25} finds
certainly inside the window put the 95% confidence limit on the start of their record at
[428.8]{.C27 .V003} Ma (figure 4*a*).
Finding rates that track collection density give [428.9]{.C27 .V009} Ma, or [428.5]{.C27 .V010} Ma with
plant-bearing collections removed, and a 25 Myr decline into the past gives [431.6]{.C27 .V011} Ma. If
finding fossils became steeply harder with age (the 10 Myr decline), the method sets no limit as far
back as [469.4]{.C27 .V007} Ma, the edge of our data. That history, however, predicts a spread of fossil
ages unlike the one observed, however the uncertain ages are assigned[]{.C49}. The 25 Myr decline passes
the shape test under one assignment of ages when all [50]{.C49 .V107} possible finds are counted
(*p* = [0.067]{.C49 .V104}), and fails under every assignment of the [48]{.C49 .V069} finds certainly
inside the window (best *p* =
[0.035]{.C49 .V106}).

**(f) Counting fewer independent finds moves the limit past 431.2 Ma.** Treating each region and stage as one find
([19]{.C30} finds) gives [432.4]{.C30} Ma, and counting only the curated oldest records ([15]{.C30}
finds) gives [434.05]{.C30} Ma, both at a steady finding rate. Shifting stage boundaries by their quoted
uncertainties, in the scenarios tested, moves the main limit between [427.31]{.C50 .V062} and
[430.42]{.C50 .V063} Ma (electronic supplementary material, S5.3).

**(g) Older vascular-plant dates fit if early fossils were found less often, or recognisable bodies
appeared later.** In the main scenario, halving the finding rate before the oldest find moves the limit
to [431.0]{.C53 .V108} Ma, still younger than the 431.2 Ma maximum. Ancestors at 444–452 Ma, spanning
the Morris et al. mean dates and the Harris et al. interval, fit if fossils were found
[8]{.C48 .V054}–[12]{.C48 .V055} times less often before the oldest find than after it, with
recognisable bodies appearing with the ancestor (figure 4*b*). Counting by region and stage, or only the
curated oldest records, [2.4]{.C48 .V056}–[4.5]{.C48 .V057} times suffices, and 431.2 Ma already fits a
steady rate. At a steady finding rate, the same ancestors fit if recognisable bodies appeared at least
[15–23]{.C33} Myr after them. Smaller reductions combine with shorter delays (figure 4*b*).

**(h) One Ordovician find moves the broader land-plant limit.** Sporangial fragments with in situ
cryptospores from the Ghaba-1 borehole, Oman, dated to the Katian on later correlation
[@wellman2003FragmentsEarliestLand], form one land-plant body find. Adding them moves the steady-rate
limit for all land-plant body fossils from [434.5]{.C28} to [455.2]{.C40 .V022} Ma, and the enlarged
set fails the shape test under all six histories[]{.C49}. The fragments came from palynological
preparation of borehole material, whereas most later finds are compressions collected from bedding
planes [@edwards1979RecordsFertileRhyniophytina].

## Discussion

The early record allows older ancestors, and the analyses state what each would require. In the Murzuq
Basin, the older samples exclude a steady, well-delivered trilete supply at the Llandovery level through
the later Ordovician; lower delivery, lower local supply or spore producers outside the catchment all
fit. At a steady finding rate, or one tracking collection density, the 95% confidence limit on the start of the
recognisable crown vascular-plant record lies near [429]{.C27 .V018} Ma, and counting fewer independent finds
puts it at [432.4–434.05]{.C30} Ma. For each way of counting, a lower earlier finding rate, later recognisable
bodies or both fit ancestors older than these limits; the models give the size of each requirement in
aggregate.

Several processes could have made early plants scarcer in the record. Producers may have been rarer,
more geographically restricted, concentrated where little terrestrial sediment accumulated, or preserved
in intervals that have been searched less. Body size, cuticle and lignified tissue could also raise the
chance that a body is preserved, found and recognised. Branched sporophytes and conducting tissue were
acquired stepwise along the lineages being dated [@kenrick1997OriginEarlyEvolution]. The early branched
sporophyte *C. barrandei* is preserved in marine sediments [@libertin2018SporophytesPolysporangiateLand],
and the Oman fragments show that small sporangia can survive [@wellman2003FragmentsEarliestLand]. The
models give the combined reduction a candidate age requires; the contribution and plausibility of each
process remain open.

Silvestro et al. [@silvestro2015RevisitingOriginDiversification] modelled genus-level PBDB plant
occurrences with a joint Bayesian model of preservation and diversification, and estimated the origin of
vascular plants at a mean of 433.5 Ma (95% highest posterior density 449.0–424.0 Ma). They estimate when
the earliest vascular-plant genus lineage arose, with a fitted, varying preservation rate; we limit the
start of the recognisable record of a declared crown category under assumed finding-rate histories, then
ask what older ancestors require. The two analyses target different quantities from partly shared data
and test different assumptions. CladeDate builds calibration densities directly from fossil times
[@claramunt2022CladedateCalibrationInformation], and Hooper et al. link spore-character reconstruction
to revised calibrations. We make the maxima's observational requirements explicit, limiting local spore
supply and sizing the reductions in finding rate, or the delays, that fit older vascular-plant dates.

**Conditions.** The spore limits assume that the Llandovery spores are indigenous, that older and
younger samples were reported comparably, that ages are uniform within stages, and that samples are
independent apart from a shared well effect. Three exploratory checks of that dependence, untested on
simulated data, give [0.0995]{.C51 .V058} with a separate Llandovery rate for each well and no well
effect, about [0.4]{.C51 .V081} when samples are weighted by well and rock unit, and
[0.185–0.365]{.C07} (varying supply) with one well left out at a time. Linking the local spore record to
ancestors requires that the earliest crown embryophytes produced recognisable trilete spores, that the
trait persisted and that producers lived in the catchment (electronic supplementary material, S6).

The body-fossil limits and shape rejections assume independent ages on a continuous scale; fixed
finding-rate histories, cutoff, timescale, inventory and counting rules; and an observation process
whose reported age ranges always contain the true ages, including whether each find falls inside the
window. Geological age ranges carry no demonstrated joint coverage; if the probability that any range
misses its true age is $\delta$, the test's error rate is at most $\alpha+\delta$, where $\alpha$ is its
level for exact ages. Simulations with exact ages
gave coverage of [0.941–0.958]{.C34}, falling to about [0.78]{.C34} when paired duplicate horizons were
counted as independent (electronic supplementary material, S5.6). The step scenario was chosen after
seeing the data. Linking the vascular-plant limit to ancestors requires that the oldest finds belong to
the crown group, and a specified delay before recognisable bodies.

Published evidence could narrow these requirements. Records of searched Silurian terrestrial beds, with
effort and empty beds included, would help constrain early finding rates, and compilations of rock units
could supply proxies for the terrestrial rock available at each age [@peters2018MacrostratPlatformGeological].
A second complete spore sequence outside polar Gondwana, with older samples reporting no triletes,
younger samples reporting them and counted target and control grains, would test whether the Libyan result is general; a fully
reported marine sequence older than 515.5 Ma, with comparable recovery documented, would help quantify
the oldest maximum's absence argument. Finer, independent ages for the Yea (Victoria, Australia) and
Bathurst Island (Arctic Canada) horizons, which hold the oldest possible crown vascular-plant finds,
would sharpen the body-fossil limit [@earp2024NotanopliidBrachiopodsAssociated;
@kotyk2002MorphologicallyComplexPlant]. Characters placing the oldest branched sporophytes on the
lycophyte or euphyllophyte side of the vascular crown could change which fossils enter the category, as
well as their link to the ancestor.

## Conclusion

A fossil maximum based on absence combines expectations about ancestral characters with the chance of
finding their remains in older rocks. Clock builders can state each maximum's character, geographical
and finding-rate assumptions, and justify the probability assigned to older ages against them
[@parham2011BestPracticesJustifying; @yang2005BayesianEstimationSpecies]. Testing alternative defensible
maxima then shows how much each assumption moves the timescale, and sampling the joint age prior without
molecular data, then comparing its effective marginal ages with the posteriors, shows what the molecular
data add [@brown2017PastSureTense]. The same separation of the evidence, the chance of finding it and its
link to the ancestor applies to any maximum argued from absence. Older dates for living land plants fit
the fossil record if early plants were found several times less often, or recognisable features evolved
later.

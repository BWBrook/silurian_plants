## The puzzle

Every forest, grassland and crop descends from a few small plants that colonised the land hundreds of millions of years ago. Those plants changed the air, the soils and the climate. Yet scientists disagree by tens of millions of years about when the ancestor of today's land plants lived.

A *molecular clock* compares the DNA of living plants and works out how long their lineages have been diverging. It dates the shared ancestor of living species, which may be younger than the first plants on land. Clocks need fossils to set their time scale, above all *maximum ages*: arguments that a much older group would probably have left recognisable fossils in the rocks examined. Move the maximum and the clock's answer moves with it. Three recent clock studies place the ancestor of all living land plants 515–474 million years ago (Ma) or 457–450 Ma, depending on their maximums, and the ancestor of living *vascular* plants, which have internal plumbing, between about 452 and 428 Ma [@morris2018TimescaleEarlyLand; @harris2022DivergentEvolutionaryTrajectories; @hooper2026ReconstructingSporeEvolution].

## Why early plants are hard to date

The oldest traces of land plants are tough, microscopic spores. Spores appear in the Middle Ordovician, fragments of spore capsules in Late Ordovician Oman, and branched plants, often only millimetres tall, in the Silurian. Missing fossils in older rocks can mean the plants were absent, or that small, woodless and perhaps rare plants were seldom preserved, found and recognised. A maximum age has to argue for the first reading.

## What we did

We traced the three maximum ages that divide the clocks (515.5 and 459.1 Ma for land plants, 431.2 Ma for vascular plants) to their original fossils, ages and arguments. We modelled spore records from Libyan boreholes, where none of [28]{.C01} samples older than about 443 Ma reports three-rayed (*trilete*) spores while [51 of 59]{.C02} younger samples do; [10]{.C45} of the older samples are broadly dated and may reach back beyond 459 Ma. From how [48]{.C25} dated groups of vascular-plant fossils are spread through time, we calculated how far back that record could reach under several histories of how often plants left fossils that were later found.

## What we found

1. **The oldest maximum (515.5 Ma) rests on a qualitative argument.** Older marine rocks preserve tough algal cysts and no land-plant spores, so its authors read the spores' absence as real[]{.C46}. How many spores reached those seas, and how hard anyone looked, remain unmeasured.
2. **The Libyan rocks limit sustained, well-delivered spore input.** A steady flow of spores into these rocks from 459.1 Ma fits only below about a tenth of the later flow ([0.091]{.C24}); lower input, poor delivery or plants living elsewhere all fit. This treats samples from one well as linked; weighting samples by well and rock unit instead gives an exploratory figure near [0.4]{.C51 .V081}. The result covers mainly the later Ordovician in one polar basin.
3. **Vascular plants can be older if early fossils were scarcer, or recognisable later.** At a steady recording rate, the 95% older limit on their body record is about [429]{.C27 .V001} Ma, or [432–434]{.C30} Ma if the finds are counted as fewer independent places. A retrospective scenario that halves the earlier rate reaches only [431.0]{.C53 .V108} Ma. Dates of 444–452 Ma fit if such fossils were found [8]{.C48 .V094} to [12]{.C48 .V095} times less often before the oldest find than after it ([2.4]{.C48 .V096} to [4.5]{.C48 .V097} times if counted cautiously), or at a steady rate if recognisable bodies appeared at least [15–23]{.C33} million years after the ancestor.
4. **One find can move the limit by 20 million years.** Counting the tiny Late Ordovician spore-capsule fragments from Oman moves the limit for land-plant bodies back by about 20 million years[]{.C40}.

## What it means

The vascular-plant record leaves room for older ancestors if early plants were several times scarcer in the record, or gained their recognisable features later. The Libyan spores leave room for older ancestors whose spores reached that basin sparsely or not at all. Plants that were uncommon, local, small or seldom searched for would leave exactly such a record; our calculations give the size of the requirement, and its biological plausibility remains open. Clock builders can state the fossil, geographical and recording assumptions behind each maximum and test how alternative maxima change their dates. In short: older dates for living land plants fit the fossil record if early plants were found several times less often, or recognisable features evolved later.

## In brief

*Sengelia radicans* is a prostrate, mat-forming lycophyte from the Beartooth Butte Formation at Cottonwood Canyon, Wyoming, dated late Lochkovian to early Pragian. It is reconstructed from more than 1,000 compression specimens in which leafy stems, root-bearing axes and roots are found attached and in growth position. Stems up to 34 mm wide were probably too thinly built to stand; every K-branch made a downward-growing root-bearing axis that anchored the shoot, and fine roots arose from those axes. Dense monospecific stem mats alternate with flood siltstones, and sporangia are very scarce (33 on 20 stem fragments). The describers read these together as a plant that persisted by clonal spreading and by regrowth after burial, with infrequent sexual reproduction. That reading is an inference, not a measurement. The card sits against the height-and-spore-output narrative: it shows a low, spreading, anchored strategy established by the late Lochkovian to early Pragian, documented in one section.

## Morphology and anatomy

Matsunaga and Tomescu (2017, over 400 rock specimens from five collections: USNM, DMNH, FMNH, KU and HPH) recognise three axial organs. **Leafy stems** are 3-34 mm wide (mean 15 mm, n = 567), carry helical deltoid leaves of 1-7 mm, and have a central stele only 1-2 mm thick. Continuous fragments are up to 25 cm long and the combined length of stem fragments measured is 35 m. The cortex is preserved as very thin carbonaceous film even in the widest stems, so it was probably low-density parenchyma or aerenchyma without supporting tissue. **Root-bearing axes** are leafless apart from sparse reduced leaves at the base, 2-8 mm wide (mean 4.4 mm, n = 452), with a stele of 0.5-1.5 mm; segments run from 3 mm to 13 cm (about 14 m in total among 177 specimens; Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016). **Roots** are 0.4-0.7 mm thick, arise only on root-bearing axes alternately or oppositely, at tuft spacings of 2-40 mm (mean 11 mm, n = 161), fork apically up to five times and reach at most 20 mm from the axis (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016, 2017). A total of 1,131 stem and axis segments were measured (606 leafy stems, 380 horizontal and 145 vertical root-bearing axes) and 615 root tufts were counted on 310 axes. The n values for widths and the segment counts are not reconciled in the paper, and the 2017 text calls the 615 "roots" where the 2016 text says "root tufts".

Branching is exclusively K-branching: two closely spaced dichotomies that leave two arms diverging at about 180°, each fed by a thick vascular trace leaving the stele at about 90°. Every K-branch yields one leafy stem and one narrower root-bearing axis; the lateral stem is generally smaller than the main stem; branching may be successive or reiterated; intervals run from 2 cm to at least 25 cm. Buds (K-branch primordia with two apices), incomplete K-branches (one arm and a dormant bud) and complete K-branches all occur, complete ones being commonest; buds occur on stems of all sizes (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016, 2017).

**Sporangia** are round to slightly ovoid, 2.3-3.7 mm across, cauline and sessile, with a distal-margin dehiscence line in some. Only 33 were recovered, on 20 stem fragments, and 14 of them on just three of those fragments. No clear vascular supply was found and no spores were recovered (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017).

Anatomy beyond the stele is thin. Tracheids are known only as iron-oxide lumen casts with transverse wall bars; the thickening type cannot be resolved. Stomata are anomocytic and evenly spaced on stems (about 7-8 per mm²), rare on root-bearing axes and not seen on leaves. Root-bearing axes have a thick cuticle, which the authors take to mean they were not the absorbing organs (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016, 2017).

## Age and distribution

Cottonwood Canyon only, on one level. All specimens come from a section 1.5-1.8 m thick at points within 10 m of one another, because access at the canyon does not allow collection elsewhere (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017). The age is late Lochkovian to early Pragian on McGregor's unpublished palynology reported by Tanner (1983) and on fish biostratigraphy (Elliot and Johnson 1997), both via Matsunaga and Tomescu (2017). Noetinger et al. (2021) then analysed 28 samples from about 1 m of the plant-bearing sequence, including three from Sengelia mats, and found a late Lochkovian or Pragian age by the ranges of its palynomorphs (abstract and data README; the range table was not available). On data/timescale.csv the Lochkovian-Pragian boundary is at 413.02 Ma and the Pragian ends at 410.62 Ma; the quoted "about 411 Ma" matches the GTS2012 boundary value and is not a measured age. Both statements give a Lochkovian-Pragian window and differ only in the upper bound. The type locality of the formation, at Beartooth Butte, is mid to late Emsian by the same palynological method (Noetinger et al. 2021, README), so the formation name does not date a Sengelia find. See [Early lycopsids](early-lycopsids.md) for the group's record.

## Affinity and controversies

Leaves with a single vein, K-branching and cauline sporangia fit the Drepanophycales, but the describers set Sengelia apart on leaf size and shape, exclusively K-branching architecture and, most of all, a rooting system of specialised root-bearing axes (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017, Table 1). Two Drepanophycus species, *D. devonicus* and *D. minor*, resemble it more than they resemble *D. spinaeformis* and were transferred to Sengelia on frequent K-branching, short leaves and root-bearing axes from K-branches. Matsunaga and Tomescu (2017) point to root-like axes beside *D. spinaeformis* in Scotland (Rayner 1984) and in the Middle Devonian (Schweitzer 1980) but leave them unassigned for lack of organic connection. The [Drepanophycus spinaeformis](drepanophycus-spinaeformis.md) card carries the Chinese *D. qujingensis* rhizome data; its rhizomes resemble Sengelia's rooting structures, and Matsunaga and Tomescu (2017) infer comparable floodplain colonisation and sediment stabilisation by drepanophycalean lycophytes in both regions (Xue et al. 2016, via Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017). The roots are the contested organ: the lateral appendages are most similar to the roots of living *Lycopodium* and *Selaginella* but cannot be tested against the traditional criteria in compressions. The authors treat them as roots borne on modified stems and conclude that lycophyte roots arose as a novel organ, not from branches (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016). Claisse et al. (2025) did not include Sengelia, so no cladistic test of its position exists in the sources I read.

## Ecology and physiology

**Growth form.** Stems were creeping, with at most the apical regions erect (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017). The argument is taphonomic and anatomical: very thin compressions, narrow steles in wide stems, and root-bearing axes at every K-branch, which keep the whole shoot system close to the ground. Stems carried the stomata and were probably the main photosynthetic organ, with leaves small relative to stem surface.

**Rooting.** Root-bearing axes grew downward as well as horizontally, above and below the surface. Vertical axes cross bedding planes and extend at least 12 cm below the mat layer (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016, specimens DMNH 29594, HPH 210 and others), root tufts spread horizontally from both vertical and horizontal axes, and the roots probably lacked a gravitropic response. The authors infer that the axes anchored the plant and explored the substrate and the roots were the main absorptive organ, a functional reading that has no direct physiological test (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016). Horizontal and vertical axes are often parallel and do not cross; the authors suggest, with an explicit "may", that this reflects kin recognition or avoidance and present it as a possibility.

**Stand-level natural history (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016, 2017).**

- *Mats.* Interwoven leafy stems and axes lie in dark, finely laminated shales with other plant taxa absent and without transport debris; several mat horizons occur within less than 1 cm of rock in some blocks (HPH 77) and several stem layers separated by about 5 mm of sediment in others (HPH 231).
- *Alternation.* The mat shales alternate with hard-cemented siltstones that hold transported plant debris and fish plates, and that are penetrated by vertical root-bearing axes from the mat above. The authors read the sequence as repeated colonisation of fresh flood sediment and burial by the next flood; the time scale is stated as indefinite, and no number of cycles or years is given.
- *Submergence.* Encrusting microconchids occur on 325 stem fragments (60%) and 109 horizontal root-bearing axes (30%), and on no vertical axes, so mats were submerged for some time before burial, while the other 40% of stems were not. A mottled zone about 40 cm thick under the uppermost mat records water-table fluctuation well below the plant surface, and the authors conclude that submergence was temporary.
- *Rooting zone.* In the uppermost siltstone, a zone about 12 cm thick with abundant vertical axes is strongly oxidised, which they read as a pedogenic effect of rooting.

**Life history (inference).** Four observations support clonal predominance: stems that could not stand and formed dense mats, frequent and sometimes reiterated K-branching, numerous dormant buds, and rare sporangia, 33 on 20 stem fragments of the more than 1,000 specimens, with 14 on three fragments and the others in loose fertile zones. The authors link the buds to regrowth through overlying sediment after burial and the strategy to the 'turfing in' habit proposed for early land plants (DiMichele and Hook 1992, via Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017). Their conclusion is a life history adapted to survival after floods, dominated by clonality, with infrequent sexual reproduction. What is not shown: no clone, genet or ramet has been identified; mat extent and individual boundaries cannot be resolved in compressions; the sporangium count is limited by preservation, and collecting effects are not assessed; and the authors call evidence that the mats were clonal a matter for further study. Noetinger et al. (2021, abstract) find only one zosterophyll-assignable spore type for zosterophylls and lycophytes together, and the Sengelia sporangia yielded none, so reproductive output has no independent measure here. No fungal association, gametophyte or spore is reported in the sources read.

## Why it matters for modelling

- **Height as the counterpoint.** Sengelia is a large-bodied plant by axis width (mean 15 mm, up to 34 mm) that was nonetheless low and prostrate, with apices at most erect. Axis width is therefore not a proxy for height. Set against *Asteroxylon*, reconstructed at about 30 cm (Early lycopsids card, citing the Asteroxylon card), it separates stem thickness from stature.
- **Persistence under disturbance.** The stratigraphy records repeated recolonisation of flood deposits by the same species in a section about 1.5-1.8 m thick. It shows that the strategy persisted through several flood-burial episodes at one place. It does not show that it outcompeted taller strategies: nothing in the sources compares growth, survival or fitness with the other taxa at Cottonwood Canyon, and those taxa are absent from the mats, not demonstrated to be excluded by them.
- **Belowground architecture.** One root-bearing axis per K-branch, depths of at least 12 cm, root tufts at a mean 11 mm spacing and roots reaching at most 20 mm. These are inputs for anchorage and substrate-exploration terms and for plant-sediment interaction; rooting density and oxidation mottling are highest in the siltstone beneath the uppermost mat (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016).
- **A measurable fertile fraction, not a rate.** The sporangium counts bound how often fertile material was preserved; they do not give spores per plant per year.
- **Contrast with Drepanophycus qujingensis.** Both are rooted floodplain colonists that spread clonally (see the Drepanophycus card), and Matsunaga and Tomescu (2017) suggest that comparable colonisation was potentially widespread among drepanophycalean lycophytes. Each card's numbers belong to its own species and site.

## Model transfer

**Chosen to contrast:** clonal-growth and height. Sengelia is one of the few Early Devonian plants with a whole-plant concept that integrates growth habit, life history and growth environment (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017); its stand-level natural history points to spreading, anchoring and recovery after burial rather than to height, and its wide stems show that width is not stature.

- **Can transfer:**
  - *Prostrate, non-self-supporting growth form:* stems 3-34 mm wide (mean 15 mm, n = 567) forming dense, interwoven monospecific mats in laminated shales; Cottonwood Canyon, Beartooth Butte Formation, over 400 rock specimens (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017). Height itself is not measured.
  - *Branching rule:* exclusively K-branching on leafy stems, intervals 2 to at least 25 cm, one leafy stem and one root-bearing axis per K-branch, lateral stems smaller than main stems; same specimens (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016, 2017).
  - *Rooting architecture:* root-bearing axes 2-8 mm (mean 4.4 mm, n = 452), vertical ones reaching at least 12 cm below the mat (DMNH 29594, HPH 210; Matsunaga and Tomescu 2016); roots 0.4-0.7 mm, tufts 2-40 mm apart (mean 11 mm, n = 161), up to 20 mm long, up to five dichotomies; 615 tufts on 310 axes among 177 specimens. The depth is a minimum set by the outcrop and by what was preserved.
  - *Disturbance cycle:* colonisation of flood sediment, burial, and recolonisation, in a section 1.5-1.8 m thick; microconchids on 325 stem fragments (60%), a water-table mottle zone about 40 cm thick; the number of cycles and their duration are not given (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017).
  - *Sporangia as an observation:* 33 sporangia, 2.3-3.7 mm, on 20 stem fragments, 14 on three; among more than 1,000 specimens (Matsunaga and Tomescu 2017).
  - *Age:* late Lochkovian to early Pragian, or late Lochkovian or Pragian, at Cottonwood Canyon only.
- **Cannot transfer:**
  - *Height, vertical growth or any stature trade-off:* no height, growth rate or competitive outcome was measured; 25 cm is a fragment length.
  - *Clone size, clone age, vegetative spread rate or ramet turnover:* inferred from stand structure only; no clone or genet was identified.
  - *Reproductive allocation, spore number, spore size or heterospory state:* no spores were recovered; the sporangium count is limited by preservation, and collecting effects are not assessed.
  - *Water-transport properties:* tracheid wall type is unresolved and the stele is 1-2 mm in stems up to 34 mm wide; do not assign G-type or any conductivity.
  - *Fungal nutrition, gametophyte and sporophyte independence:* not reported in the sources read.
  - *Generality across sites or time:* one canyon, one section, one level, specimens within 10 m; the type locality of the formation is a different age.
  - *Congeners:* *S. devonica* (Eifelian) and *S. minor* (Givetian) have stalked sporangia and other dimensions, and *D. qujingensis* is a separate species and site; do not pool their sizes or rooting data with Sengelia without a `from:` marker.
  - *Kin recognition or avoidance in root foraging:* a possible reading of axis parallelism, not demonstrated.

## Open questions

- Whether the stem mats were clones: compressions cannot resolve individuals, and the authors point to the spatial relations of root-bearing axes as a possible route.
- Whether sporangium rarity reflects low fertility, a seasonal or short fertile phase, or preservation; how many fertile fragments exist beyond the 20 reported.
- The spores, spore size and homospory or heterospory of Sengelia; the full Noetinger et al. text may show whether any dispersed spore type belongs to it.
- Whether the roots are true roots by lycophyte criteria and whether the root-bearing axes anchored mats against flood scour.
- Number, thickness and duration of the colonisation-burial cycles in the 1.5-1.8 m section, and how much time the section spans.
- A conference abstract cited by Noetinger et al. (2021) reports gametophytes from the Beartooth Butte Formation (Caruso et al. 2011; title only, not read).

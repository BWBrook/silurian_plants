# Charter

Status: **Stage 0, orientation** (opened 2026-10-26). No research question is chosen yet.

## The idea

Barry's starting point, in his words (chat, 2026-10-26): "a simulation modelling project on the
evolution of early land plants (late Ordovician to early Devonian), but it's a very nascent
thought". He frames it as "a long-term development project that might prove infeasible or
uninteresting, but is worth … at least exploring", with no hurry, and with the structure left
free to evolve.

## Why this interval

Between roughly 470 and 393 Ma, land plants went from a dispersed-spore record with no
recognisable bodies, through centimetre-scale leafless axes, to lycopsids and euphyllophytes with
leaves, roots, secondary-growth precursors and metre-scale height. Nearly every innovation that
defines vascular plants appears in that window: desiccation-tolerant spores, cuticle and
stomata, lignified conducting cells, branching sporophytes independent of the gametophyte,
apical dominance, leaves and roots. The same plants are implicated in changes to weathering,
atmospheric CO2 and O2, river form and soil. The record is unusually uneven, with abundant spores
and a few exceptional deposits (the Rhynie Chert above all) set against long gaps in
megafossils, so most explanations of the pattern remain verbal. That combination of a major
evolutionary transition, rich constraints and few quantitative tests is the case for modelling.

## What would make it worth doing

A framing earns a modelling programme if it meets all four tests:

1. **Discriminating evidence.** Competing hypotheses predict outcomes that the fossil, proxy or
   experimental record can tell apart: trait sequences, timing, size distributions, diversity
   or disparity patterns, geochemical signals.
2. **Novelty.** The model adds something the precedents (`docs/precedents.md`) do not: a
   mechanism, a coupling, a confrontation with data, or a reanalysis that changes a conclusion.
3. **Tractability.** The data can be assembled and the model run with resources on the mesh,
   without years of bespoke data collection.
4. **Interest.** Barry finds the question worth his attention.

If no framing passes tests 1 and 2 after orientation, the project is parked with a short written
account of why. That account is itself a useful output.

   Ends with a choice of framing, which is Barry's call on a recommendation from the agents.
1. **Sketch:** the smallest end-to-end model of the chosen framing, deterministic where possible.
2. **Ground:** null models, baselines and the data the model will confront (trait matrices built
   from the cards, occurrence data, proxy series).
3. **Enrich:** one mechanism at a time, each motivated by a discrepancy with data.
4. **Compare:** contrasts that discriminate between competing explanations.
5. **Synthesise:** what is robust, what depends on representation, and what is next.

Stages are a guide, not approval gates. Barry's operating principle applies: reversible work
proceeds and is shown afterwards; first public release, submission and outward-facing messages
are his decisions.

taxa/zosterophylls.md:95:Zosterophylls are leafless plants whose bivalved sporangia sit laterally on the axis, in spikes or rows, often on H- or K-branched rhizomes. Banks (1968) erected them as a separate subdivision, and for decades they were the presumed ancestors of the club-moss lycopsids. The latest analysis finds them a grade, with the spiny Sawdoniaceae as sister group of the lycopsids (Claisse et al. 2025). Their late Ludlow fossils are the oldest secure crown-tracheophytes (Hooper et al. 2026). Claisse et al. call them the dominant tracheophytes at the start of the Devonian, though rhyniophytoids dominated cooler Lochkovian floras (Capel et al. 2023). The group offers measured size and life-history spread. Members: [Zosterophyllum](zosterophyllum.md), [Sawdonia ornata](sawdonia-ornata.md), [Renalia hueberi](renalia-hueberi.md) (excluded from the 2025 analysis as incertae sedis).
taxa/zosterophylls.md:99:The defining state (Lycophytina) is a laterally attached, bilaterally symmetrical, bivalved sporangium opening along its distal margin (Claisse et al. 2025). The 2025 matrix of 44 taxa (38 zosterophylls) shows the spread: sporangia irregular or in two rows, reniform to globose, in spikes (*Zosterophyllum*, *Bathurstia*) or scattered; naked axes or emergences; circinate tips in the Sawdoniaceae; H- and K-branching in rhizomes in a scattered set of taxa; xylem elliptical in the Sawdoniaceae. Huang et al. (2025) count at least 37 genera. *Zosterophyllum* axes are 0.3-5.1 mm wide and photosynthetic, with each extra millimetre of diameter adding 31 mm2 of surface per 10 mm of axis. In *Z. shengfengense* downward root-like axes of about 20 mm carried only about 3% of biomass (Hao et al. 2010).
taxa/zosterophylls.md:107:Banks (1968, 1975) defined the group by leafless axes with lateral sporangia and exarch xylem, with six genera, and derived lycopsids from it. The sampled sets differ: 7 genera in Gensel (1992), 23 species in Kenrick and Crane (1997), 8 species in Hao and Xue (2013), 19 species in Nibbelink and Tomescu (2022) and 33 genera in Claisse et al. (2025), and the definition stretched as new genera arrived. Gensel (1992) and Kenrick and Crane (1997) put zosterophylls in a grade paraphyletic to the lycopsids, with Zosterophyllopsida a smaller clade of circinate, two-rowed forms; Hao and Xue (2013) recovered a monophyletic class excluding lycopsids (both as summarised by Claisse et al. 2025). Nibbelink and Tomescu (2022) and Claisse et al. (2025) support the grade. In Claisse et al., Lycophytina splits into a small basal clade and a large one, in which the Sawdoniaceae and lycopsids are sister groups and sister to a clade with the barinophytes; *Zosterophyllum myretonianum* sits elsewhere in a basal group, so the genus is polyphyletic. Parsimony and Bayesian trees share only 43.6% of nodes. Exarch xylem maturation, short stalks and thickened dehiscence lines, listed by Kenrick and Crane as lycophyte synapomorphies, are not supported.

## In brief

Zosterophyllum is the type genus of the zosterophylls: leafless plants with naked axes, H- or K-branched basal rhizomes, and stalked, bivalved sporangia borne laterally in terminal spikes. Its earliest record is late Ludlow (Ludfordian, 425.0-422.7 Ma), it diversifies in the Lochkovian and peaks in the Pragian. The genus is probably not monophyletic, and lycopsids arose from within the zosterophyll grade, so its species show the ancestral body plan from which microphyllous lycopsids evolved. Plant and spike size differ about tenfold among coeval species, which makes it a useful system for life-history trade-offs.

## Morphology and anatomy

Species are known mainly as compressions, with axes 0.3-5.1 mm wide ending in spikes. Spikes in subgenus Zosterophyllum have helically arranged sporangia, whereas Platyzosterophyllum has sporangia oriented to one side of the axis (Gensel 1982; Huang et al. 2025). Sporangia are oval to reniform, and in Z. baoyangense measure 1.6-2.0 mm by 0.9-1.4 mm, with 5-10 per spike; Z. yunnanicum and Z. australianum have a thickened sporangial border (Huang et al. 2025; the 2015 redescription of Z. yunnanicum was not accessed). Z. myretonianum has a single median strand of tracheids (Lang 1927) and was, in 1961, the earliest plant in which cuticle, stomata and complete tracheids had been clearly demonstrated (Lele & Walton 1961). Its basal region was branched mainly by H-type and related patterns, with simple dichotomy rare and the upright fertile axes seldom branched (Lele & Walton 1961). Since the axes carry stomata and there are no leaves or spines, the axes were the main photosynthetic organs (Huang et al. 2025).

The best whole-plant evidence is Z. shengfengense (Hao et al. 2010): an uprooted tuft of aerial axes 98 mm tall on a rhizome that bore upward axes and downward root-like axes about 20 mm long. This is observed for one species; extending it to the genus is an inference.

## Age and distribution

Kotyk et al. (2002) report ten fertile plants of seven taxa, mostly zosterophylls including one Zosterophyllum, from late Ludlow beds of Bathurst Island, dated by conodonts, graptolites and brachiopods; they judged the size and complexity much greater than most contemporaneous fossils. Huang et al. (2025) treat the Yunnan species Z. qujingense as Silurian as well, but Claisse et al. (2025) describe a Pridoli gap in zosterophyll fossils; I could not reconcile the two. Diversity then rose in the Lochkovian (Z. myretonianum in Scotland; Z. shengfengense, about 413 Ma, and Z. xishanense in Yunnan), peaked in the Pragian (Z. australianum from Yunnan, Guizhou and Victoria, Z. rhenanum, Z. confertum, Z. baoyangense) and fell in the Emsian (Cascales-Miñana & Meyer-Berthaud 2014; Huang et al. 2025), a trajectory a model can be tested against. Other records are Z. cf. fertile in the Senni Beds of South Wales (Siegenian-Emsian; Edwards 1969), fertile Z. myretonianum in Scotland (Edwards 1975), Z. australianum in Victoria (Norton Gully Sandstone; McSweeney et al. 2022) and Emsian Z. divaricatum in New Brunswick (Gensel 1982). At least 20 species have been described, at least 14 from South China. The upper Xujiachong Formation, which yields many Chinese species, is dated by spores as early (but not earliest) Pragian to possibly earliest Emsian (Wellman et al. 2012).

## Affinity and controversies

Gensel (1992) argued that lycopsid microphylls and leaf-associated sporangia could be derived from zosterophyll emergences and sporangial position. Recent analyses agree that zosterophylls are a grade paraphyletic to the lycopsids (Nibbelink & Tomescu 2022; Claisse et al. 2025), whereas a 2013 analysis of the Posongchong flora recovered a zosterophyll clade (as summarised by Claisse et al. 2025). Zosterophyllum itself is not a clade: in Claisse et al. (2025) Z. myretonianum falls in a basal group with Discalis and Gumuia, far from the Sawdoniaceae that are sister to the lycopsids. Most species lack anatomy and spores, so species-level relationships are weakly tested (Huang et al. 2025).
